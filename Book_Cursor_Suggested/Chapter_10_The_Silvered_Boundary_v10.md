## §1 The Bifurcation

At certain critical moments, history bifurcates — the way a single path diverges into two branches. You walk forward along one branch or the other; whichever you take, you cannot then cross to the other without going back to the fork. The two branches are two different ways the same structure could go from a given decision point.

The framework of this book takes this picture seriously as physics. The universe is one manifold with two orientations. They are not two parallel realities in the science-fiction sense — no alternate version of the reader who took a different career lives in the other orientation. But they are two, and both are real: the framework's own structure requires it, because a mirror that were merely our own sector counted twice would supply no extra matter for the dark-matter ratio to measure. The two orientations are related by the framework's CPN symmetry, which constrains them tightly enough that they have the same matter content, the same forces, the same large-scale structure. They are mirror images in a precise mathematical sense, sharing a spatial boundary at the Janus surface at *N* = 0 and never crossing it. We occupy one. The other is real, with its own galaxies and stars and (possibly, in some loose sense) observers, all evolving under the same physics.

One point deserves italics before the picture settles, because it is where the framework's mirror differs from every mirror the reader may have met in popular physics. *The other orientation is not running backwards in time.* The mirror relation flips the orientation of the manifold's e-fold thickness — the N direction — and the emergent cosmic time is not that direction; time, in the framework, runs orthogonal to the thickness, and flipping N leaves the arrow of time untouched. Both branches age the same way, forward, together. The universe next door is not our past unspooling in reverse or a time-reversed twin; it is a same-aged sibling, sharing our forward direction of emergent time, distinguished from us only by which face of the thickness it occupies. Frameworks exist in the literature whose mirror universes run with reversed time; this is not one of them, and the difference is a structural commitment with consequences.

The previous chapter took this bilateral structure and derived the bare coupling κ₀ = 3/√(4π). An algebraic amplifier that once closed to κ = 5.50 is **withdrawn (2026-10-09)** — no deposited action. The earlier claim that 5.50 was the cosmic dark-to-ordinary ratio was already gone. Both facts travel into this chapter.

**What this chapter is now for.** The bilateral structure has consequences beyond the bare coupling — a coherence scale, an averaging law, a particular relationship to lensing and to the direct-detection programme — and those consequences are worth developing, because they are what a future account of observed cold dark matter would have to be consistent with. What the chapter can no longer do is present them as the framework's account of dark matter. It does not have one. So every section below is written at the register the framework actually occupies: here is what the geometry gives, here is what would have to be shown for it to bear on the observed dark sector, and here is what the chapter is not entitled to say. A reader who finds that unsatisfying is reacting correctly.

This was the structural claim of the framework's reading of dark matter, and it is worth stating in its old form so that the correction is legible. The matter we observe gravitationally but not electromagnetically, the framework used to say, is not a new species of particle in our orientation; it is the matter content of the *other branch*, whose curvature registers in ours through the cross-thickness coupling Chapter 9 developed. Dark matter, in that picture, was already accounted for — a consequence of living on one side of a manifold with another side.

The structure is real and the accounting claim is withdrawn. The other orientation exists in the framework, and its matter does register gravitationally in ours through the bare coupling κ₀. What has not been shown — and what three separate investigations established cannot be shown on the framework's present state — is that this is the cold, collisionless, galaxy-shaped component cosmologists measure. The conjugate sector sits at the same temperature as ours by symmetry, which makes its matter baryon-like rather than cold during the epoch that matters; the coupling transmits gravitational magnitude rather than position; and a thermally-connected mirror wrecks the radiation budget the microwave background has measured. Chapter 9 §7 carries the full two-step withdrawal. The framework therefore says, and this chapter will keep saying, that it predicts a bilateral gravitational footprint whose relation to the observed dark matter is **open**.

The image at the chapter's head — a silvered boundary no thicker than a breath, holding the weight of two orientations — is the same picture from a different angle. The bifurcation happens, on the framework's reading, exactly once, at the moment time itself switches on — at gauge birth, *t* = 0. The pre-temporal regime — call it the *root* before the fork — is octonionic, non-associative, undifferentiated; it has no orientations to distinguish, because the e-fold direction whose two faces the orientations are has not yet locked. At *t* = 0, when associativity locks in and time becomes real, that regime differentiates along the manifold's CPN symmetry into two orientations: the branch we walk along, and the branch whose gravitational weight we feel without ever being able to visit. There is no returning to the root; time has switched on, and the bifurcation is locked. After the bifurcation, the two branches run in parallel — in the same forward time — sharing the spatial boundary at *N* = 0, the silvered glass through which we read the other side's curvature via the cross-thickness coupling. The bifurcation is a temporal event (at *t* = 0); the silvered boundary is a spatial feature of the branches (at *N* = 0); they are different things. Fork and mirror are two images for the bilateral manifold, capturing different aspects of it: the fork at the bifurcation moment, the mirror at the steady-state spatial structure.

What follows is what this picture commits the framework to, and — since October 2026 — what it no longer lets the framework claim. The two are set out side by side rather than blended, because a reader deserves to see which is which.

## §2 The Empirical Case

Picture a spiral galaxy spinning in space. The stars near the edge ought to orbit more slowly than the ones near the centre — that is what visible mass alone predicts. They do not. Something invisible, roughly five times heavier than all the stars and gas put together, is holding the rotation curve flat. That something is what cosmologists call dark matter. Four independent lines of evidence tell the same story.

The first line is *galaxy rotation curves*. In the 1970s, Vera Rubin and Kent Ford measured the rotation speeds of stars in the outer regions of spiral galaxies. The speeds did not fall off the way they should have if the only mass were the visible matter — the stars and gas the telescope could see. Instead, the rotation curves stayed flat far out beyond the visible disc, as if the galaxies were embedded in a much larger, much more massive halo of something whose mass extended well past the luminous edge. The pattern was found in galaxy after galaxy. The amount of "extra" mass, in each case, was roughly five times the visible matter.

The second line is *gravitational lensing*. Light passing near a massive object is bent, by an amount that depends on the mass. Cosmological surveys map the lensing pattern around galaxy clusters and find that the total mass inferred from the lensing far exceeds the visible matter — by roughly the same factor as the rotation-curve evidence demands.

The third line is the *cosmic microwave background*. The CMB's pattern of temperature fluctuations encodes the relative amounts of ordinary matter and dark matter at the moment the universe became transparent, around 380,000 years after the gauge birth. The acoustic peaks in the CMB power spectrum are sensitive to the ratio: the relative heights of the first three peaks pin down the dark-to-ordinary ratio with high precision. Planck's measurement is 5.38 ± 0.15 — the value an earlier edition of Chapter 9 once staked the framework on, and that stake is withdrawn.

The fourth line is *large-scale structure*. The cosmic web — galaxies clustered into filaments and sheets, with vast voids between — formed under the combined gravitational influence of all the mass in the early universe, dark and ordinary. N-body simulations that include both kinds of mass reproduce the observed cosmic structure with high fidelity. Simulations with only ordinary matter do not reproduce it at all. The gravitational influence of the dark sector is required to produce the universe's structure as we see it.

These four lines converge. They give consistent values for the cosmic dark-to-ordinary ratio. They are independent measurements drawing on different physical processes at different cosmic epochs. They tell a coherent story.

What they do not tell us is *what dark matter is*. They tell us that there is mass gravitating without emitting light, roughly five times as much of it as there is ordinary matter, and — this is the part that matters for what follows — that it is cold, does not scatter, and sits where the galaxies sit. Those three properties are not add-ons; they are how the measurement works. The alternative readings, most of them invoking new particle species, have spent four decades looking for what they predict and have not found it. The framework of this book does not currently supply a reading that satisfies the three properties either, and §4 says why in the framework's own terms rather than leaving the reader to infer it.

## §3 The Particle Hypothesis That Hasn't Worked

The dominant theoretical approach to dark matter, since the 1980s, has been to postulate a new particle. The candidate particle would be massive, electrically neutral, and weakly interacting — interacting at most through the weak nuclear force and gravity, never through electromagnetism. Such a particle would gravitate without emitting light, which is what dark matter does. It would also, if produced in the early universe in roughly the right abundance, leave behind the cosmic ratio we observe.

The most popular candidate, for decades, was the *weakly-interacting massive particle* — a WIMP. WIMPs at masses ranging from roughly 10 GeV up to a few TeV would have been produced thermally in the early universe and would have settled into something like the observed dark-matter abundance through standard particle-physics processes. The picture was well-motivated, mathematically clean, and led to an obvious experimental programme: build very sensitive detectors deep underground, shielded from cosmic rays, and wait for a WIMP to scatter off an atomic nucleus and deposit a small amount of energy. The energy deposit would be tiny but detectable.

This programme has been pursued with extraordinary effort. The XENON, LUX-ZEPLIN, and PandaX experiments — among others — have each grown more sensitive by orders of magnitude over successive generations. The most recent generation can detect single WIMP-nucleus collisions with sensitivities far better than the original WIMP-mass-and-interaction-strength predictions required.

They have found nothing.

This is not a failure of one experiment. It's the consistent result across multiple experiments, multiple target materials, multiple mass ranges. The original WIMP parameter space — the range of WIMP masses and interaction strengths that the simplest theoretical models predicted — has been definitively ruled out. The remaining parameter space is parts of theory space that were never strongly motivated, kept alive by adjusting the candidate particle's mass or coupling away from where it was originally expected.

Other particle candidates — axions, sterile neutrinos, primordial black holes, asymmetric dark matter — have similar status. Each has a parameter space; some experimental programmes have ruled out parts of those spaces; the candidates have retreated into corners of theory space that are progressively less natural and less testable. The decades-long search has not produced a positive result for any candidate.

A natural reaction is to consider whether the search itself is misdirected, and earlier editions of this book had the framework take that step: if dark matter is not a particle in our orientation, no detector built in our orientation will find one, so the negative results are not failures but correct results.

**The framework has given that argument up.** It rested entirely on the identification Chapter 9 §7 withdrew. With no account of the observed dark matter, the framework has no licence to say dark matter is not a particle — it has no position either way, and an absence inferred from a withdrawn premise is not an absence anyone has earned. What stands is the plain experimental record above: the original WIMP parameter space is ruled out and the surviving theory space is less natural than it was. That is a fact about the search, not a point scored by this framework, and the chapter has no business presenting it as one. Nor would a detection now refute the framework; it would fill a gap the framework has left open, and the framework would then owe an account of how such a particle relates to the bilateral geometry.

## §4 The Other Branch

What the framework does offer is narrower: the matter content of the other orientation, whose gravitational curvature registers in ours through the cross-thickness coupling. Whether that is what anyone has been measuring is the open question this chapter keeps in front of the reader.

Recall the bilateral picture from Chapter 7. The universe is one manifold with two orientations, related by CPN symmetry, sharing the Janus boundary at *N* = 0. The bifurcation between them — the fork in §1's image — is anchored in time exactly at *t* = 0, the gauge birth moment Chapter 7 developed: before that moment, the octonionic regime had no orientations to differentiate; after, the two orientations are locked in and the bifurcation cannot be unwound. The fork is a temporal event (at *t* = 0); the Janus boundary is a spatial feature of the manifold (at *N* = 0); they are different things. We occupy one orientation. The other has its own matter — its own galaxies, its own large-scale structure — under the same physics, in the same forward direction of emergent time. Recall from Chapter 9 the cross-thickness coupling: the geometry of the manifold itself knits the two orientations together, and gravitational curvature on either orientation registers across the manifold's N-thickness in the other orientation's metric. The registration needs something bilaterally supported to register against, and the unresolved neutrino is the only species in the framework whose quantum state has support across both orientations. It is worth keeping the roles apart, because the natural misreading is the one Chapter 9 works hardest to prevent: the neutrino does not carry the coupling and nothing passes through it. The coupling is a fact about the manifold's geometry, present whether or not anything bilateral exists; the neutrino is the structure that gives it somewhere to act, and so the thing that makes it show up in the equations we measure.

What we observe gravitationally in our orientation has two contributions. The first is the curvature sourced by the ordinary matter on our side — the stars, the gas, the planets, the things our telescopes can directly observe. The second is the curvature sourced by the matter on the *other* side, registered into our orientation's metric through the cross-thickness coupling. The total gravitational signal is the sum of the two contributions. The dark-to-ordinary ratio is the ratio of these two contributions.

What the previous chapter deposits is the **bare coupling** κ₀ = 3/√(4π) ≈ 0.846, set geometrically by the Hopf fibration on the shared S³ boundary. It also described a second step — a closure whose geometric series resums the feedback of the coupling acting on its own contribution, giving κ = κ₀/(1 − κ₀) ≈ 5.50. **That amplifier is withdrawn (2026-10-09): it has no deposited action, and 5.50 is not a framework quantity.** Earlier editions ran the sentence on to "the cosmic dark-to-ordinary ratio Planck measures"; that ending went first, in October 2026, and the step that produced the number has now gone after it.

The other branch — the bifurcation image from §1 — is the framework's gentle way of pointing at this picture. There is a gravitational presence belonging to the matter on the other side of the manifold's thickness: a side whose existence is structurally forced by the framework's geometry, whose contents we cannot reach and whose light we cannot see, but whose mass registers in our gravitational equations through the cross-thickness coupling. That presence is not visible to us, it is present, and it is part of why our universe looks the way it does.

Earlier editions continued: *dark matter is not particles, not a new species hiding in the dark of our universe.* Those two sentences are gone. The framework has an orientation whose matter gravitates in ours; it does not have a demonstration that this is the dark matter, and so it has no standing to say what the dark matter is not. The reader should hold the picture and withhold the identification, which is exactly the position the framework is in.

This is a structural claim, not a metaphor. The framework's gravitational equations have specific form. The bilateral coupling has specific strength κ₀. The bare coupling κ₀ is a consequence of those commitments, not an interpretation overlaid on them. Two further things *were* overlaid and have both been removed: the claim about which observed quantity an amplified number corresponds to, withdrawn in October 2026, and then the amplifier itself, withdrawn days later for want of a deposited action. **The lesson has had to be corrected twice, and the second correction is the sharper one.** An earlier edition of this chapter said a derivation and its referent are separate things and only one of them was ever in trouble. That was true of the first withdrawal and false of the second: the amplification step was in trouble too, one step further down than anyone had looked.

## §5 The Coherence Length, and the Law of the Scatter

The bilateral reading has structural consequences beyond κ, and they are worth developing on their own terms — as properties the coupling must have, which any future referent would have to respect. The coupling acts wherever the neutrino's bilateral wavefunction gives it a structure to act on, and that wavefunction does not hold its bilateral character over unlimited distances. So the coupling inherits a scale from it — the neutrino's free-streaming scale, the reach over which an unresolved neutrino's wavefunction maintains its bilateral structure before decoherence and gravitational scattering disrupt the coherent superposition. The framework states that scale as a *coherence mass* rather than a length, because a mass is what the gravitational accounting of a system actually turns on: above it, a system is large enough to average over many independent patches of the coupling; below it, it is not. The mass itself is around four hundred trillion suns — but that figure is not a constant of the theory, and the next paragraph says why.

Four hundred trillion solar masses is a rich galaxy cluster. To put it in context: a typical dwarf galaxy holds perhaps a hundred million solar masses in total; our own Milky Way, a trillion or so; a large galaxy cluster, a few hundred trillion. The coherence mass therefore sits at the boundary between the cluster scale and the scale of the cosmic web's filaments and voids — which is the boundary the next section's prediction turns on.

Three caveats travel with that figure, and the framework states them rather than leaving them implicit. The free-streaming scale is standard neutrino physics, not an NTE derivation; what the framework once claimed as its own was the *combination* — this scaling law, around a mean of 5.50, with the transition at this scale — and **the mean has gone with the amplifier that produced it.** The *size* of the scatter within a single coherence patch is not predicted at all, and the corpus carries that amplitude as open: the framework predicts that the scatter is there and how it falls away with mass, not how large it is at the bottom. And the threshold is not a fixed number. Free-streaming scales inversely with the neutrino's mass, so the coherence mass moves as the neutrino mass moves, and the neutrino mass is not something the framework has pinned. Four hundred trillion suns is the value at a standard reference mass, not a constant the geometry hands down. A reader should hold it as an order of magnitude with a dial attached, and the surveys that test the transition will have to be read against whatever the neutrino mass turns out to be.

What this means physically: below the coherence mass, the cross-thickness coupling fluctuates significantly. The bilateral wavefunction is intact at small scales, but the coupling's contribution to the gravitational signal varies from place to place, depending on local conditions of the unresolved neutrino's distribution. Above the coherence mass, the coupling averages out. The cosmic-scale signal is the geometric mean of the framework's structural calculation; below the coherence mass, the local signal can deviate substantially from that mean.

And the averaging has a *law*, not just a direction. A system's gravitational accounting samples the fluctuating coupling across however many independent coherence patches the system spans, and averaging over independent samples is one of the oldest results in mathematics: the scatter of the average falls as one over the square root of the number of samples. Since the number of patches a system spans grows with its mass, the framework's two-sector equations deliver a specific scaling — the system-to-system scatter in the dark-to-ordinary ratio falls off as one over the square root of the mass, σ(M) ∝ M^(−1/2), above the coherence scale. The scaling shape itself is a generic statistical consequence; what once made it the framework's own was the combination — this scaling, around this mean of 5.50, with the transition at this coherence scale. Three quantities, one mechanism.

The prediction this gives the framework is sharp. *Below* the coherence mass — at galaxy and small-cluster scales — the dark-to-ordinary ratio should show wide scatter from system to system. Some systems should be dominated by dark matter; others should have very little. *Above* the coherence mass — at large-cluster and cosmic scales — the conjugate contribution should converge toward its mean, with the residual scatter shrinking as M^(−1/2). Note carefully what the framework may and may not fill in here: the **scaling** is a consequence of the coupling's structure, but the **mean is not a framework quantity at all.** It was 5.50 under an identification withdrawn in October 2026, and 5.50 itself was withdrawn days later with the amplifier. So the surviving statement is about the shape of a fall-off with no deposited value to fall to — which is a good deal less than a prediction.

This is a specific, testable structural claim, and the next section walks the comparison with observation.

## §6 Galaxies, Clusters, and the Scatter

The observational picture of dark-matter scatter, across cosmic scales, is qualitatively consistent with the framework's coherence-length prediction — with the quantitative verdict belonging to the coming surveys.

At dwarf galaxy scales — systems much smaller than the coherence mass — the dark-matter fractions show wild variation. Some dwarf galaxies are almost entirely dark matter; their visible stars are a tiny perturbation on the gravitational signal of an enormous dark halo. Others show very little dark matter; their stellar dynamics can be reproduced with not much extra mass at all. The diversity is one of the most striking and least explained observational facts about dwarf galaxies. Why should one ultra-faint dwarf have a hundred times the dynamical mass of its visible content while another, similar in luminosity and apparent size, has only twice? The standard particle-dark-matter picture attributes the diversity to environmental effects — tidal stripping, accretion history, baryonic feedback — and this is a real and active research area; the honest statement is that those explanations are worked out case by case rather than derived from the dark sector's own structure, and that none of them was predicted in advance to produce the observed range.

The framework's reading is that the scatter is structural. Below the coherence mass, the cross-thickness coupling fluctuates pointwise, depending on local conditions of the unresolved neutrino's bilateral wavefunction. Some dwarf galaxies sit at points where the local cross-thickness contribution is large, and they appear dark-matter-dominated. Others sit at points where the contribution is small, and they appear nearly free of dark matter. On this reading the scatter is the framework's prediction, not a deviation from it — though the framework should not claim the dwarf data as a passed test until the *pattern* is measured: the standard picture's environmental explanations remain live alternatives for the same qualitative facts, and what separates the two accounts is quantitative.

At galaxy-cluster scales — systems comparable to or larger than the coherence mass — the dark-matter fractions converge. The diversity that haunts dwarf galaxies essentially disappears. Galaxy clusters have dark-to-ordinary mass ratios that cluster around the cosmic value, with deviations comparable to the natural system-to-system variation in baryonic content. This is the direction the coherence-length argument predicts.

What used to convert consistency into a verdict was the law from §5, as a three-part quantitative test: the shape σ(M) ∝ M^(−1/2), around a mean of 5.50, with the transition at the coherence mass. **Two of the three legs are now gone outright rather than weakened.** The mean went twice over — first its identification with the measured ratio, then the amplifier that produced the number. The transition scale was never derived — it is standard neutrino free-streaming physics, adopted, and it moves with a neutrino mass nobody has pinned. What is left is the shape alone, and the shape alone is a weak test: many averaging processes fall as the square root of sample size, and the framework's claim to that behaviour was always its *combination* with the other two legs rather than the behaviour itself.

So the honest statement is this. Rubin's Legacy Survey, Euclid and the spectroscopic surveys will map dark-matter fractions across hundreds of millions of systems, and that will be a magnificent dataset. The framework currently cannot say what it predicts those maps will show, because the quantity being mapped is the one whose relation to the framework's geometry is open. Earlier editions presented this as one of the framework's sharpest coming tests. It is not, at present, a test of the framework at all, and saying so is cheaper than letting the reader discover it.

This is the kind of thing the framework is supposed to be doing: making specific structural predictions whose observational signature is unambiguous, and putting itself in a position where future data either confirms or breaks the prediction.

## §7 Lensing and the Bullet Cluster

One of the most striking pieces of evidence for dark matter is the *Bullet Cluster*. In 2006, observations of two galaxy clusters that had recently collided showed a clean separation between the gravitational mass distribution (mapped through gravitational lensing) and the ordinary baryonic mass distribution (mapped through X-ray emission from the hot gas in the clusters). The hot gas, which is the bulk of the ordinary matter in galaxy clusters, had collided and been slowed by electromagnetic interactions; the dark mass, which appeared not to interact electromagnetically with the hot gas, had passed through the collision and continued on its original trajectory.

This observation is often called definitive evidence for the existence of dark matter. The gravitational mass is somewhere; the electromagnetically-interacting matter is somewhere else; the two distributions have separated, exactly as they would if the gravitational mass were not subject to the same electromagnetic interactions that slowed the hot gas.

Earlier editions of this chapter gave the framework a reading of this observation, and that reading has to be withdrawn — not because of anything the Bullet Cluster showed, but because of something the framework says about itself. The reading went: the gravitational mass that passed through unimpeded is the curvature contribution of the other orientation's matter, which cannot interact electromagnetically with our hot gas because photons live in their own orientation, so the cross-thickness signal behaves exactly like a non-interacting dark mass — tracking the other side's matter distribution and lensing our background light accordingly.

The difficulty is in the word *tracking*. The framework's deposited description of the coupling is that it transmits gravitational **magnitude, not location**: each orientation feels how much mass the other has, not where it is. That property is not incidental — it is the premise §4's derivation of κ runs on. But the Bullet Cluster is a measurement of *where*. It is interesting precisely because two mass distributions ended up in different places, and a lensing map is a map. A contribution that carries no positional information cannot produce a positional offset, and the old reading quietly assumed it could.

So the framework's present position on cluster collisions is that **it does not have a reading of them**. This is a real gap and a new one: the location-blindness that makes κ derivable appears to be in tension with any account of spatially-resolved dark-sector observations, and the framework has not reconciled the two. The corpus carries the coupling's location-blindness in two separate papers, so the tension is internal rather than a matter of interpretation, and it is recorded as an open question rather than resolved in this chapter's favour. What the chapter will not do is keep the old paragraph with a hedge in front of it.

One thing the Bullet Cluster does establish, and it establishes it for everybody: whatever the dark mass is, it does not interact electromagnetically the way the hot gas does. On the framework's geometry that much would be structurally forced rather than postulated, since there is no electromagnetic coupling between orientations at all. But "consistent with the part that everyone's account gets right" is not a result, and the paragraph above explains why the framework cannot currently claim the rest.

Subsequent observations of cluster collisions — MACS J0025.4, El Gordo, and several others — show the same structural fact: gravitational and baryonic mass separate during collisions, with the gravitational mass behaving as though decoupled from our orientation's electromagnetism. They sharpen the problem rather than soften it. Every one of them is a positional measurement, and there are now several.

## §8 What's Coming

The chapter has set out the bilateral manifold's structural consequences, and been explicit about which of them are currently predictions and which are not.

The framework makes no prediction about direct detection. Earlier editions had it predicting continued nulls, on the reasoning that there is no dark-matter particle in our orientation to detect; that prediction is withdrawn with the premise under it. Underground experiments will go on refining their limits, and the framework's standing does not now turn on what they find — neither a null nor a detection would confirm or refute it. Giving up a bet one appeared to be winning is the least comfortable line in this chapter, and it is the one most worth keeping.

On the survey side, the structural expectation that survives is the shape of the fall-off: whatever the conjugate sector contributes to a system's gravitational accounting should average down as σ(M) ∝ M^(−1/2) above the coherence mass. The mean that the fall-off was supposed to converge on is withdrawn, and the coherence mass itself is adopted neutrino physics that moves with a mass nobody has measured. So the framework offers one leg of what was a three-legged test, and §6 has already said that one leg does not stand up on its own.

On lensing and cluster collisions the framework's position is the open one §7 arrived at. Those observations will continue to show the dark sector behaving as though decoupled from our electromagnetism, and the framework has no difficulty with that part. What it owes, and does not yet have, is an account of how a location-blind coupling produces spatially-resolved structure at all. Until that is supplied, further lensing results are neither support nor refutation — they are additions to a question the framework has not answered.

The framework makes no prediction about what CMB-S4 will measure for the cosmic dark-to-ordinary ratio. It will measure it at roughly twice Planck's precision, and the framework has no stake in the answer. The long discussion of a 0.36 per cent residual at the bare-coupling level, which earlier editions carried here, has gone with the comparison that generated it: a residual is the difference between a prediction and a measurement of the same quantity, and there was no such pair.

Dark matter, in the framework's reading, is not a new species we have not yet found. It's the gravitational signature of geometry the framework has had all along.

The other branch, in the bifurcation image from §1, is the path of the universe we did not walk — the branch that diverged from ours at *t* = 0, when time itself switched on, and that has been evolving in parallel ever since, in the same forward time as ours. In the framework's structural account, it's the orientation we do not occupy — sharing the Janus boundary at *N* = 0, evolving under the same physics, hosting its own galaxies and stars and structure, never reachable by any signal we could send. The chapter ends where the framework actually stands: there is another orientation, its matter gravitates in ours, the geometry fixes a **bare** coupling κ₀ = 3/√(4π) for that contribution — and the step that turned κ₀ into a cosmic ratio is withdrawn, so the framework does not currently say how much. Nor has anyone shown that any of it is the dark matter astronomers measure. The silvered boundary holds the weight of both orientations. What the sky weighs, and whether the two weights are the same weight, is the question the next edition of this chapter would most like to be able to answer.

Whether the framework reads the universe correctly is, ultimately, what the next decade of observations will settle.
