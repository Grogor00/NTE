## §1 The Bifurcation

At certain critical moments, history bifurcates — the way a single path diverges into two branches. You walk forward along one branch or the other; whichever you take, you cannot then cross to the other without going back to the fork. The two branches are two different ways the same structure could go from a given decision point.

The framework of this book takes this picture seriously as physics. The universe is one manifold with two orientations. They are not two parallel realities, exactly — no alternate version of the reader who took a different career lives in the other orientation as a separate fiction. The two orientations are related by the framework's CPN symmetry, which constrains them tightly enough that they have the same matter content, the same forces, the same large-scale structure. They are mirror images in a precise mathematical sense, sharing a spatial boundary at the Janus surface at *N* = 0 and never crossing it. We occupy one. The other is real, with its own galaxies and stars and (possibly, in some loose sense) observers, all evolving under the same physics.

One point deserves italics before the picture settles, because it is where the framework's mirror differs from every mirror the reader may have met in popular physics. *The other orientation is not running backwards in time.* The mirror relation flips the orientation of the manifold's e-fold thickness — the N direction — and emergent proper time is not that direction; time, in the framework, runs orthogonal to the thickness, and flipping N leaves the arrow of time untouched. Both branches age the same way, forward, together. The universe next door is not our past unspooling in reverse or a time-reversed twin; it is a same-aged sibling, sharing our forward direction of emergent time, distinguished from us only by which face of the thickness it occupies. Frameworks exist in the literature whose mirror universes run with reversed time; this is not one of them, and the difference is a structural commitment with consequences.

The previous chapter took this bilateral structure and used it to derive a specific number — the dark-matter-to-baryon ratio — that the framework's structural commitments force to be 5.50, in agreement with what cosmologists measure. The derivation was the chapter's empirical centrepiece. But the bilateral structure does more than produce one number. It reframes the entire dark-sector picture — galaxy rotation curves, gravitational lensing, the cosmic web, the negative results from direct-detection experiments. This chapter takes the framework's reading into that broader phenomenology.

This is the structural claim of the framework's reading of dark matter. The matter we observe gravitationally, but not electromagnetically, is not a new species of particle in our orientation. It's the matter content of the *other branch* — the orientation we do not occupy, whose curvature registers in our orientation through the cross-thickness coupling Chapter 9 developed. Dark matter is, in this picture, already accounted for. It's what the framework predicts must exist, given that we live on one side of a manifold that has another side.

The image at the chapter's head — a silvered boundary no thicker than a breath, holding the weight of two orientations — is the same picture from a different angle. The bifurcation happens, on the framework's reading, exactly once, at the moment time itself switches on — at gauge birth, *t* = 0. The pre-temporal regime — call it the *root* before the fork — is octonionic, non-associative, undifferentiated; it has no orientations to distinguish, because the e-fold direction whose two faces the orientations are has not yet locked. At *t* = 0, when associativity locks in and time becomes real, that regime differentiates along the manifold's CPN symmetry into two orientations: the branch we walk along, and the branch whose gravitational weight we feel without ever being able to visit. There is no returning to the root; time has switched on, and the bifurcation is locked. After the bifurcation, the two branches run in parallel — in the same forward time — sharing the spatial boundary at *N* = 0, the silvered glass through which we read the other side's curvature via the cross-thickness coupling. The bifurcation is a temporal event (at *t* = 0); the silvered boundary is a spatial feature of the branches (at *N* = 0); they are different things. Fork and mirror are two images for the bilateral manifold, capturing different aspects of it: the fork at the bifurcation moment, the mirror at the steady-state spatial structure.

What follows is what this picture commits us to, observationally — and where the framework either succeeds in matching what cosmologists have observed, or fails.

## §2 The Empirical Case

Picture a spiral galaxy spinning in space. The stars near the edge ought to orbit more slowly than the ones near the centre — that is what visible mass alone predicts. They do not. Something invisible, roughly five times heavier than all the stars and gas put together, is holding the rotation curve flat. That something is what cosmologists call dark matter. Four independent lines of evidence tell the same story.

The first line is *galaxy rotation curves*. In the 1970s, Vera Rubin and Kent Ford measured the rotation speeds of stars in the outer regions of spiral galaxies. The speeds did not fall off the way they should have if the only mass were the visible matter — the stars and gas the telescope could see. Instead, the rotation curves stayed flat far out beyond the visible disc, as if the galaxies were embedded in a much larger, much more massive halo of something whose mass extended well past the luminous edge. The pattern was found in galaxy after galaxy. The amount of "extra" mass, in each case, was roughly five times the visible matter.

The second line is *gravitational lensing*. Light passing near a massive object is bent, by an amount that depends on the mass. Cosmological surveys map the lensing pattern around galaxy clusters and find that the total mass inferred from the lensing far exceeds the visible matter — by roughly the same factor as the rotation-curve evidence demands.

The third line is the *cosmic microwave background*. The CMB's pattern of temperature fluctuations encodes the relative amounts of ordinary matter and dark matter at the moment the universe became transparent, around 380,000 years after the gauge birth. The acoustic peaks in the CMB power spectrum are sensitive to the ratio: the relative heights of the first three peaks pin down the dark-to-ordinary ratio with high precision. Planck's measurement is the value the previous chapter staked the framework on: 5.38 ± 0.15.

The fourth line is *large-scale structure*. The cosmic web — galaxies clustered into filaments and sheets, with vast voids between — formed under the combined gravitational influence of all the mass in the early universe, dark and ordinary. N-body simulations that include both kinds of mass reproduce the observed cosmic structure with high fidelity. Simulations with only ordinary matter do not reproduce it at all. The gravitational influence of the dark sector is required to produce the universe's structure as we see it.

These four lines converge. They give consistent values for the cosmic dark-to-ordinary ratio. They are independent measurements drawing on different physical processes at different cosmic epochs. They tell a coherent story.

What they do not tell us is *what dark matter is*. They tell us only that there is mass that gravitates without emitting light, and that there is roughly five times more of it than there is ordinary matter. The framework of this book is one possible structural reading of that empirical situation. The alternative readings — most of them invoking new particle species — have spent the past four decades looking for what they predict, and have not found it.

## §3 The Particle Hypothesis That Hasn't Worked

The dominant theoretical approach to dark matter, since the 1980s, has been to postulate a new particle. The candidate particle would be massive, electrically neutral, and weakly interacting — interacting at most through the weak nuclear force and gravity, never through electromagnetism. Such a particle would gravitate without emitting light, which is what dark matter does. It would also, if produced in the early universe in roughly the right abundance, leave behind the cosmic ratio we observe.

The most popular candidate, for decades, was the *weakly-interacting massive particle* — a WIMP. WIMPs at masses ranging from roughly 10 GeV up to a few TeV would have been produced thermally in the early universe and would have settled into something like the observed dark-matter abundance through standard particle-physics processes. The picture was well-motivated, mathematically clean, and led to an obvious experimental programme: build very sensitive detectors deep underground, shielded from cosmic rays, and wait for a WIMP to scatter off an atomic nucleus and deposit a small amount of energy. The energy deposit would be tiny but detectable.

This programme has been pursued with extraordinary effort. The XENON, LUX-ZEPLIN, and PandaX experiments — among others — have each grown more sensitive by orders of magnitude over successive generations. The most recent generation can detect single WIMP-nucleus collisions with sensitivities far better than the original WIMP-mass-and-interaction-strength predictions required.

They have found nothing.

This is not a failure of one experiment. It's the consistent result across multiple experiments, multiple target materials, multiple mass ranges. The original WIMP parameter space — the range of WIMP masses and interaction strengths that the simplest theoretical models predicted — has been definitively ruled out. The remaining parameter space is parts of theory space that were never strongly motivated, kept alive by adjusting the candidate particle's mass or coupling away from where it was originally expected.

Other particle candidates — axions, sterile neutrinos, primordial black holes, asymmetric dark matter — have similar status. Each has a parameter space; some experimental programmes have ruled out parts of those spaces; the candidates have retreated into corners of theory space that are progressively less natural and less testable. The decades-long search has not produced a positive result for any candidate.

A natural reaction is to consider whether the search itself is misdirected. The framework of this book takes that step seriously. If dark matter is not a particle in our orientation, then no detector built in our orientation will find one. The negative results are not failures. They are correct results: there is nothing in our orientation to find.

## §5 The Coherence Length, and the Law of the Scatter

The framework's reading of dark matter has specific testable predictions beyond κ = 5.50. The cross-thickness coupling, mediated through the unresolved neutrino's bilateral wavefunction, has a *coherence length* set by the neutrino's free-streaming distance — the typical scale over which an unresolved neutrino's wavefunction maintains its bilateral structure before decoherence and gravitational scattering disrupt the coherent superposition.

This scale is roughly forty megaparsecs — about a hundred and thirty million light-years.

To put that in context: a typical dwarf galaxy is a few thousand light-years across. A typical large galaxy like our own Milky Way is about a hundred thousand light-years across. A typical galaxy cluster is a few million light-years across. A typical large-scale structure (a filament or supervoid in the cosmic web) is tens to hundreds of millions of light-years across. The framework's coherence length sits roughly at the boundary between galaxy-cluster scale and large-scale-structure scale.

What this means physically: below the coherence length, the cross-thickness coupling fluctuates significantly. The bilateral wavefunction is intact at small scales, but the coupling's contribution to the gravitational signal varies from place to place, depending on local conditions of the unresolved neutrino's distribution. Above the coherence length, the coupling averages out. The cosmic-scale signal is the geometric mean of the framework's structural calculation; below the coherence length, the local signal can deviate substantially from that mean.

And the averaging has a *law*, not just a direction. A system's gravitational accounting samples the fluctuating coupling across however many independent coherence patches the system spans, and averaging over independent samples is one of the oldest results in mathematics: the scatter of the average falls as the square root of the number of samples. Since the number of patches a system spans grows with its mass, the framework's two-sector equations deliver a specific scaling — the system-to-system scatter in the dark-to-ordinary ratio falls off as one over the square root of the mass, σ(M) ∝ M^(−1/2), above the coherence scale. The scaling shape itself is a generic statistical consequence; what makes it the framework's own is the combination — this scaling, around this mean of 5.50, with the transition at this coherence scale. Three quantities, one mechanism.

The prediction this gives the framework is sharp. *Below* the coherence length — at galaxy and small-cluster scales — the dark-to-ordinary ratio should show wide scatter from system to system. Some systems should be dominated by dark matter; others should have very little. *Above* the coherence length — at large-cluster and cosmic scales — the ratio should converge toward 5.50, with the residual scatter shrinking as M^(−1/2), down to the width of measurement uncertainty at the largest scales.

This is a specific, testable structural claim, and the next section walks the comparison with observation.

## §6 Galaxies, Clusters, and the Scatter

The observational picture of dark-matter scatter, across cosmic scales, is qualitatively consistent with the framework's coherence-length prediction — with the quantitative verdict belonging to the coming surveys.

At dwarf galaxy scales — systems much smaller than the coherence length — the dark-matter fractions show wild variation. Some dwarf galaxies are almost entirely dark matter; their visible stars are a tiny perturbation on the gravitational signal of an enormous dark halo. Others show very little dark matter; their stellar dynamics can be reproduced with not much extra mass at all. The diversity is one of the most striking and least explained observational facts about dwarf galaxies. Why should one ultra-faint dwarf have a hundred times the dynamical mass of its visible content while another, similar in luminosity and apparent size, has only twice? The standard particle-dark-matter picture attributes the diversity to environmental effects — tidal stripping, accretion history, baryonic feedback — and this is a real and active research area; the honest statement is that those explanations are worked out case by case rather than derived from the dark sector's own structure, and that none of them was predicted in advance to produce the observed range.

The framework's reading is that the scatter is structural. Below the coherence length, the cross-thickness coupling fluctuates pointwise, depending on local conditions of the unresolved neutrino's bilateral wavefunction. Some dwarf galaxies sit at points where the local cross-thickness contribution is large, and they appear dark-matter-dominated. Others sit at points where the contribution is small, and they appear nearly free of dark matter. On this reading the scatter is the framework's prediction, not a deviation from it — though the framework should not claim the dwarf data as a passed test until the *pattern* is measured: the standard picture's environmental explanations remain live alternatives for the same qualitative facts, and what separates the two accounts is quantitative.

At galaxy-cluster scales — systems comparable to or larger than the coherence length — the dark-matter fractions converge. The diversity that haunts dwarf galaxies essentially disappears. Galaxy clusters have dark-to-ordinary mass ratios that cluster around the cosmic value, with deviations comparable to the natural system-to-system variation in baryonic content. This is the direction the coherence-length argument predicts.

What converts consistency into a verdict is the law from §5. The framework does not merely predict "scatter below, convergence above"; it predicts the *shape* of the convergence — σ(M) ∝ M^(−1/2) around a mean of 5.50 with the transition near forty megaparsecs. As the Vera C. Rubin Observatory's Legacy Survey, the Euclid space telescope, and the future spectroscopic surveys map the dark-matter content of hundreds of millions of systems across the full range of scales, the distribution of dark-matter fractions will either settle into that specific pattern or it will not. If the scaling appears with the predicted shape, mean, and transition scale, the framework's mechanism has passed a three-part quantitative test. If the scatter fails to fall as M^(−1/2), or the transition sits at a substantially different scale, or the convergence targets a different mean, the framework's specific claim breaks — and the standard environmental account inherits the field.

This is the kind of thing the framework is supposed to be doing: making specific structural predictions whose observational signature is unambiguous, and putting itself in a position where future data either confirms or breaks the prediction.

## §7 Lensing and the Bullet Cluster

One of the most striking pieces of evidence for dark matter is the *Bullet Cluster*. In 2006, observations of two galaxy clusters that had recently collided showed a clean separation between the gravitational mass distribution (mapped through gravitational lensing) and the ordinary baryonic mass distribution (mapped through X-ray emission from the hot gas in the clusters). The hot gas, which is the bulk of the ordinary matter in galaxy clusters, had collided and been slowed by electromagnetic interactions; the dark mass, which appeared not to interact electromagnetically with the hot gas, had passed through the collision and continued on its original trajectory.

This observation is often called definitive evidence for the existence of dark matter. The gravitational mass is somewhere; the electromagnetically-interacting matter is somewhere else; the two distributions have separated, exactly as they would if the gravitational mass were not subject to the same electromagnetic interactions that slowed the hot gas.

The framework's reading of the Bullet Cluster is consistent with this picture, but with a different ontological claim. The gravitational mass that passed through the collision unimpeded is the curvature contribution from the matter on the other orientation, registered into our orientation through the cross-thickness coupling. The matter on the other orientation is unaffected by electromagnetic interactions with our orientation's hot gas — there is no electromagnetic coupling between orientations at all, since photons live in their own orientation. So the cross-thickness gravitational signal, in our orientation's measurements, behaves exactly as if it were a non-interacting dark mass: it tracks the gravitational potential of the other orientation's matter distribution, it does not interact with our hot gas, it lenses our background light according to its registered mass.

The Bullet Cluster is, in the framework's reading, a direct demonstration that dark matter does not interact with our orientation's electromagnetism. This is what the framework predicts: dark matter, being curvature from the other orientation, has no electromagnetic coupling in our orientation. The observed separation is consistent with this. It's also consistent with the standard particle-dark-matter picture; the framework does not claim that the Bullet Cluster uniquely confirms its reading. What the framework claims is that the Bullet Cluster does not contradict its reading, and that the absence of any electromagnetic interaction with our orientation's matter is structurally forced rather than postulated.

Subsequent observations of cluster collisions have produced similar pictures. The MACS J0025.4 cluster, the El Gordo cluster, and several others have each shown the same structural fact: gravitational mass and baryonic mass separate during collisions, with the gravitational mass behaving exactly as it would if it were structurally decoupled from our orientation's electromagnetism. The framework's reading is consistent with all of these.
