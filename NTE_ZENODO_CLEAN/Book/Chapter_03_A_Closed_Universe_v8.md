## *Closed Without an Outside*

> *A surface that closes on itself in three dimensions — no inside to enter, no outside to escape to. The geometry is everywhere, and there is nowhere else.*

---

The previous two chapters arrived at a structural claim: the photon's null-time and the entanglement of two laboratory measurements are aspects of one geometric object — one object appearing as two — and the geometry that produces both is the geometry of an S³. This chapter takes that claim and asks the obvious next question — what does it mean to live on an S³? What kind of universe is that, and how is it different from the universe most of us were taught to picture?

The honest answer is that S³ is one of the harder geometric objects to picture, because our visual system evolved to work with the kind of three-dimensional space we walk through, and S³ is something else. The good news is that we don't need to picture it directly. We can build up our understanding by analogy with simpler cases, and by the end of the chapter the picture will be clear enough to support the rest of the book. The mixed news is that the most natural mental picture — the one that usually shows up in popular science writing about higher dimensions — is *almost* right, but is often presented in a way that misleads. We'll need to set the framing straight before going further.

## The Picture That Almost Works

When physics writers explain the idea that our universe might be the surface of a higher-dimensional sphere, they often reach for the balloon analogy. Imagine an inflating balloon, the writer says. We live on the surface of the balloon. As the balloon inflates, the surface stretches, and points painted on it move apart. That's why distant galaxies are receding from us. The balloon is the universe.

This is a useful picture, and the framework will use it. It captures expansion as a property of the surface itself rather than as motion through a pre-existing space. It explains why every observer sees themselves at the centre of the recession — every point on a balloon is equally central to the inflation. The framework needs both of these features.

What the analogy can mislead you into, if you take it too literally, is the picture of *what's inside the balloon*. The literal balloon has air inside, a rubber skin, and a person doing the blowing. None of those features carry over to the universe. The framework's universe is not a thin skin around a region of air, and there is no "outside" from which something could be pumping it up.

The fix is to remember what the analogy is actually doing. It's asking you to picture a creature confined to the surface of a sphere, and to notice what that creature's geometry looks like as the sphere expands. The interior of the balloon — whether it contains air or solid rubber or a brass band playing Sousa marches — is mathematical scaffolding that the analogy doesn't depend on. For the creature on the surface, the surface is everything.

This is the framing we need. Balloon, ball, soap bubble, sheet of paper — any of these works as the analogy, provided you remember the rule: look at the surface. The interior is mathematically present (every closed surface bounds an interior region; that's a fact of topology), but physically irrelevant to the creature on the surface. Our universe is the surface; the surface is the entire universe, and the surface is what the physics describes.

## Looking at the Surface

Imagine an ant living on the surface of a ball. The ant is small, the ball is large from the ant's perspective, and the ant has no awareness of any direction except along the surface. It can walk forward, backward, left, right — any direction within the surface — but it has no sense of "up" or "down" out of the surface. For the ant, those words do not name directions. They are not unavailable; they are not concepts.

If the ant walks in what seems to it like a straight line, eventually it comes back to where it started. The surface is closed — finite in total area, but with no edge or boundary. The ant cannot walk off the ball, not because something stops it but because there is no off in any direction the ant can move. From within the ant's experience, the surface extends in every direction without limit.

Asked *"what is inside the ball?"*, the ant would have no good answer — not because the ant is ignorant, but because *inside the ball* is not a place the ant can describe in terms of its own world. The interior is a higher-dimensional structure that does not exist in the ant's geometry.

We are the ant, scaled up by one dimension. Our universe is the surface. We can walk forward, backward, left, right, up, down — any direction within the surface — but there is no further direction perpendicular to the surface, because perpendicular-to-the-surface is not a direction within the universe's geometry. The four-dimensional region the S³ bounds exists as topology, but is not a physical place. The surface is everything physics needs.

## Building Up by Analogy

With the ant in hand, let us walk through the dimensional ladder.

A *circle* — what mathematicians call S¹ — is a closed one-dimensional space. It has length but no width. It's finite (you can measure its total circumference) but boundless (a creature confined to it could walk forever in either direction without hitting an end).

A *sphere* — S² — is a closed two-dimensional space. Our ant lives on one. It has area but no thickness. It's finite (you can measure its total surface area) but boundless (the ant can walk forever).

A *three-sphere* — S³ — is a closed three-dimensional space. It has volume but no... well, here the analogy runs out of words, because we don't have a fourth-dimensional concept to add. S³ has volume, but it's closed in the same sense the circle and the sphere are closed: finite total volume, but no edge or boundary. A creature inside it — and we are such creatures — would experience the space locally as ordinary three-dimensional space, but if it travelled far enough in any direction it would (in principle) come back to where it started.

This is the picture. The universe we live in is, locally, three-dimensional space. It looks Euclidean for any reasonable distance scale, the way the surface of the earth looks flat for any walking distance. But globally, the universe is closed. It does not extend to infinity. It curls around on itself in a way our intuition cannot directly visualise, but which is mathematically straightforward. There is no edge of the universe. There is also no interior of the universe in the sense of a region we could in principle reach — perpendicular-to-the-surface is not a direction within the universe's geometry.

## What Expansion Means

There is one more thing the picture needs to do before the framework can use it: it needs to be moving. The universe is expanding. Hubble's observations in the 1920s established this, and successive measurements over the past century have refined the rate but never overturned the fact. Distant galaxies are receding from us, and from each other, and the further away they are the faster they recede.

In the standard textbook treatment, this expansion is sometimes presented as if galaxies were moving outward into pre-existing empty space. The framework rejects this picture, and so does most modern cosmology if you read carefully. There is no pre-existing space the galaxies move into. The expansion is happening to space itself.

In the S³ picture, the expansion has a clean geometric meaning. The S³ has a scale — a typical distance between any two points, set by the geometry. As the universe expands, that scale grows. Distances between fixed points on the S³ increase, not because the points are moving but because the metric is changing. If you painted two dots on the surface of a balloon and inflated it, the dots stay at the same locations on the rubber, but the rubber itself is stretching, so the distance between the dots grows. This is what the balloon analogy gets exactly right.

The framework parameterises the expansion not by time but by a number called the e-fold count, written *N*. Each e-fold is one factor of *e* (about 2.7) by which the universe has grown. After two e-folds, the universe is e² ≈ 7.4 times larger than at the start; after sixty e-folds, it's e⁶⁰ ≈ 10²⁶ times larger. *N* is a pure number — a count of how much expansion has occurred — and it requires no clock to define. You don't need to know what time is in order to count expansions.

A reader who follows cosmology may want to flag a concern at this point. Current observation has the universe consistent with *flat* to within roughly a part per thousand. The Planck satellite's measurements give a curvature density Ω_K consistent with zero, with experimental error too small for the framework's S³ closure to hide in. So is the framework's commitment to a closed S³ in tension with what we observe?

It's not, and the framework's structure gives a clean reason why. Chapter 1 established that *t* is the expansion counted, not a second thing the expansion gives rise to. There is no temporal starting point for *t* in the conventional Big Bang sense — no first moment, no zero on a clock that pre-existed the universe. There is only the geometric count of expansion *N*, going back through the framework's full structural arc. The universe has been expanding through a huge run of e-folds — far more than enough to make the S³ radius, at our cosmic moment, vast on any scale we can probe locally.

And an effectively infinite radius is, locally, flat. A small sphere is sharply curved everywhere on its surface. A large sphere is curved less steeply. The S³ we live on has been expanding for so long that, at every distance scale we can measure, the radius is so large that the curvature is not detectable. The universe looks flat to us — just as it would if it were genuinely infinite. We are the ant on a ball that has been growing — large enough now that, at the ant's scale, the ground beneath its feet looks flat. The framework does not need to postulate that the radius is large; the radius is large *because* the expansion has been running for so long. Observed flatness is what the framework's structure predicts, not a coincidence the framework has to be compatible with.

This much follows from the framework's structural commitment. There is also an observational anchor that lands the same conclusion via a different route.

Even leaving the framework's argument aside, our access to the universe is bounded. The *Hubble radius* is the proper distance at which space is receding at exactly the speed of light, and it is worth being careful here because it is routinely mistaken for a wall. It is not one: galaxies beyond it recede faster than *c* — not by moving through space at superluminal speeds, but because the space between us and them stretches faster than light can cross it — and we nonetheless see a great many of them, because light emitted out there can be handed inward from one stretch of space to the next until it reaches a region receding more slowly than it travels. The surface that really does bound what we can ever see or signal is the *cosmological event horizon*, further out, and what lies beyond it is genuinely gone from our causal future.

What this gives us is a hard observational limit either way. Whatever the global geometry of the universe is, we can only ever measure inside a bounded patch — and that patch is small enough that, even if the S³'s radius weren't effectively infinite by the framework's argument, the curvature would still be undetectable inside it. The framework's structural argument is the primary claim; the bounded-patch argument is the conservative anchor. Both converge.

The framework's first sharp distinguishing empirical commitment — the dark-matter ratio κ — comes later. What flatness gives us here is consistency plus one standing commitment, and the commitment is worth stating carefully, because it is smaller than it first looks and more honest for being so.

Consistency first: the closed S³ picture is the structure the framework needs, and observation does not refute it. Near-perfect flatness is also what the standard picture expects, so agreement here distinguishes nothing at all.

The commitment is the closure's *sign*. A closed three-sphere of finite radius cannot be exactly flat — exact flatness is a different geometry, and it is the one the framework's whole surface ontology rules out. So the framework is committed to spatial curvature of the closed kind: not zero, and not open. If precision cosmology ever established an open universe at significance, the framework would break outright, and not at the level of one derivation — there is no closed-S³ reading of an open universe, so the ontology itself would go. Current data sit comfortably inside the consistent range and impose no tension.

Three things keep this from being more than it is, and the framework states all three rather than the first. The falsifier is one-sided: confirming the closed sign would not confirm the framework, because every closed cosmology predicts exactly the same thing. It is not a distinguishing test, and it does not join the handful of measurements on which the framework's case actually rests. And it predicts the sign but not the magnitude — the long e-fold run drives the curvature toward values plausibly far below anything an instrument will reach, and curvature measurements are in any case entangled with the dark-energy sector, so the number may stay out of range indefinitely.

Which leaves the commitment in an unusual position, and an instructive one. Here is a thing that would prove the framework wrong, recorded on the understanding that we may never get to find out. A framework should say what would refute it even where refutation is unlikely to be reachable — especially there, because that is where the temptation to stay quiet is strongest.

This matters because of the central claim from Chapter 1: time is not the primary *parameter*. *N* is — and that is a statement about which coordinate can be written without circularity, not about which phenomenon is more real. What we experience as time and what the geometry does as it expands are one structure, with the boundary expansion field |Φ_NTE| parameterising the rate at which cosmic time accumulates per e-fold. Chapter 4 will work this out in detail. For now, hold the picture: *N* is the geometric count of expansion, *t* is what that count amounts to for anything embedded in it with |Φ_NTE| setting the rate, and the universe is an expanding closed three-dimensional space whose radius has grown so vast through a huge run of e-folds that it appears, locally, flat.

## What "The Other Side" Means

A reader who has been working with the ant analogy may notice a question. The ant lives on a ball. The ball has an antipodal point — the point on the opposite side of the ball from wherever the ant is. If the ant walks long enough in any direction, eventually it reaches the antipode. So if our universe is an S³, doesn't it have an antipodal point too? What's there?

The answer is yes, geometrically — and we should be careful about what this does and does not imply. Here are the two things worth saying.

The first is geometric. The antipodal point on an S³ is not a different region or universe — just the point geometrically furthest from where you are, on the same closed surface. In the framework's picture, that distance is effectively infinite at our cosmic moment; the entire antipodal hemisphere lies far beyond the **cosmological event horizon** — the bound introduced above, and the one that actually applies. The antipode is part of the same universe, made of the same fabric, governed by the same laws, and almost certainly empty of any structure that matters for our local physics.

The second concerns a misreading worth heading off. Later chapters will claim that what looks to us like *dark matter* — the invisible mass shaping galaxies — is best understood as gravitational coupling across a *bilateral* structure of the manifold, with one particular particle's unfinished identity serving as the geometric handle that makes the coupling register. A reader thinking about antipodal points might infer that the mechanism involves matter at the antipodal spatial point. It does not. The bilateral structure that does the work is a thickness or orientation feature of the manifold itself, not a separation between two spatial regions.

But here a subtler doubt may surface. If the S³ has two sides, surely they cannot both have the same geometry? Imagine the surface of a sphere. From the outside, the surface bulges outward — convex. From the inside, the surface curves toward you — concave. So if our universe is the S³, and the S³ has two sides, shouldn't one of them be convex and the other concave?

The intuition is sharp, and the framework wants you to hold it for a moment — and then ask one further question. When you imagine the convex face and the concave face of a sphere, what vantage are you imagining from? You are seeing the sphere from outside it, in three-dimensional space. The convex face curves away from you; the concave face curves toward you. The distinction is real, but it's being made from outside the sphere itself. Now: do we have that vantage for our universe? The framework's S³ has no ambient four-dimensional space it sits inside. There is no outside. Everything physics actually measures is measured from inside, with the metric the surface carries.

And from inside — the only vantage that exists for inhabitants like us — the curvature of a sphere is the same from both faces. This is a theorem of differential geometry, not a framework-specific claim. The intrinsic curvature an inhabitant can measure by adding up triangle angles or watching parallel lines gives the same answer whichever face you are on, at any radius. The inhabitant of the convex face and the inhabitant of the concave face would write down the same number. So the two sides of our S³ have the same geometry, from the only vantage we have — not just statistically the same (at large scales the universe could look uniform for many reasons) but geometrically the same, from any local measurement an inhabitant could perform. The convex/concave intuition was tracking an extrinsic distinction visible only from outside an embedding the framework doesn't admit.

What the two sides differ in, then, is something other than curvature.

The closest everyday analogy is the two sides of a sheet of paper. Put an ant on one side. The ant lives its life on that face — walks around on it, explores its terrain, has no awareness of any direction perpendicular to the paper because perpendicular is not a direction within the ant's geometry. Now put another ant on the other side of the same paper. From the perspective of either ant, the other is nowhere to be found by walking — they can explore their own face forever and never reach each other. But the two ants are not far apart in the way that two distant points on the same face are far apart. They are adjacent at every point, separated only by the paper's thickness — a separation in a direction neither ant can move along.

Now imagine that one ant has mass, and that mass exerts gravity. The gravitational influence of the massive ant doesn't have to travel along the surface to reach the other ant; it can act through the thickness of the paper. The other ant feels gravity coming from somewhere it cannot reach by walking, somewhere not on its surface, somewhere it might be tempted — wrongly — to call *invisible matter*.

The two sides of the framework's manifold are like this, scaled up by one dimension. The bilateral structure that does the dark-matter work is a thickness-direction feature of the closed three-dimensional manifold. Matter on one side is gravitationally coupled to matter on the other side, but neither side can see the other by any local observation any creature confined to its own side could make. This is the structural setup that the framework's cross-thickness dark-matter mechanism will exploit — the neutrino's role in it, — not carrying the coupling, not mediating it, but being the structure that makes it show up in the equations describing what we measure —, is a story for the chapters that develop it carefully. The other side is not a bookkeeping device, and not our own matter counted twice. It is real, with its own matter, gravitating in its own right — which is exactly why there is something for the ratio κ to measure.

What this means for now: the antipodal-spatial geometry of S³ is real and interesting and does not, by itself, do the dark-matter work. The geometry that does the dark-matter work is a different feature of the same closed three-dimensional manifold, and later chapters on dark matter are where it lands. The point of mentioning the antipodal point in this chapter is just to flag that closed manifolds have this structure, that the framework is happy to acknowledge it, and that closure does not mean "trapped" — the universe is one closed three-dimensional space, finite in volume but with no edge or boundary, and every point on it is part of the same manifold.

Both sides of the S³, all the matter, all the radiation, all the geometry — lives on one closed three-dimensional space. There is no inside, no outside. There is the S³, and the S³ is everything.

## Where This Leaves the Photon and the Entangled Pair

The Chapter 1 photon and the Chapter 2 entangled pair land cleanly under the Chapter 3 picture.

Chapter 1 established that the photon is not a thing in flight — it's the geometric connection between emission and absorption, a single object with length to anyone measuring it from outside and no ruler along its own. The Ch 3 picture gives this its structural location. The photon is on the boundary of the expanding S³, riding the expansion as the manifold unfolds. The forty million light-years you measure between the emitting star and your eye is the amount the manifold has unfolded between the photon's two ends — not territory the photon traverses, but the expansion drawing the two events apart while the photon stays on its boundary. The photon-line records that they remain, geometrically, one object.

Chapter 2 established that an entangled pair is also one object — a single geometric feature, not two coordinating particles. The Ch 3 picture gives this its structural location too: the feature is one structure on the same closed 3D manifold. The two particles are not separated by territory the structure has to span; they are endpoints of one feature, and the feature does not require anything to travel between them because the "between" the reader's intuition reaches for is not what's there. There is no vantage belonging to the structure itself from which its two endpoints could be seen as one — that is the point, and it is why the one-object claim is a claim about the object rather than about a privileged observer of it. From the laboratory vantage, which is the only one there is, they appear as two particles in different rooms. Two appearances, one object, and nothing between the endpoints for a signal to travel on.

Both phenomena are the same structural move: one object appearing as two, with the appearance every bit as real as the object. The photon's null-time is the first instance; entanglement is the second. Both are features of the same expanding S³ — the photon's two ends drawn apart in spatial-and-temporal extent, the entangled pair's two ends in spatial extent — and in neither case is there anything along the inside of the link for the reader's intuition to put a traveller on.

## Towards Time and Expansion

The picture is now in place. Closed three-dimensional space. No interior, no exterior. Expansion as a change in the metric, parameterised by *N*. Photons riding the wavefront. Entanglement as a feature of the same surface with nothing accumulating along it. Time bound to that expansion through the boundary expansion field — but the nature of the binding, and its rate, is the piece we have not yet worked out.

The next chapter takes that piece up directly. If time and expansion are one structure rather than two, what exactly is the relation between them, and why is *N* the coordinate that can be written down first? What role does the boundary expansion field play in setting the rate? And what does this tell us about the difference between the world we observe and the world the framework actually describes?

The epigraph at the head of this chapter pointed at the structural posture we have just established. We have only the surface to work with — the geometry of being on a closed manifold that is moving. We do not have access to the road beneath it, or the space outside it. We have only the surface, and the question of where it's going.

The framework of this book takes a similar posture, scaled up by one dimension. We do not have access to whatever the universe is "going through" in the sense of the deeper geometry. We have the universe — the S³ surface, expanding, carrying us along with it — and the question of what we can learn about its structure from inside it. The next chapter takes a closer look at the moving part of that picture.
