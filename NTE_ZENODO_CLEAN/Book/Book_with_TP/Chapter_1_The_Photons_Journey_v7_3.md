## Preamble: The Fact We Forget to Question

There is one staggering fact about the photon that is often glossed over in introductory physics: **the photon experiences null-time.** In the standard curriculum, this is usually stated as a curiosity, accepted as a mathematical consequence of relativity, and promptly forgotten so the student can move on to calculating refraction indices.

But some of us cannot move past it. We cannot simply *accept and move on*, because the implications are too massive to ignore. If a photon experiences no time, then from its own frame, the moment it's emitted from a star forty million light-years away and the moment it strikes your eye are the same event. It does not travel. It simply connects.

When we push harder into this *just-accept-it* fact, the standard walls of physics begin to dissolve. Entanglement becomes less mysterious. The hidden geometry of the universe begins to unfold in front of us. And what was a curious mathematical detail about light becomes the seed observation from which an entire framework grows.

This book follows that seed.

## A Note on Method

Before going further, a word about how this book proceeds. Richard Feynman, in *QED*, asked why all theories of physics look so similar in their structure, and offered three possibilities. The first was that physicists have limited imagination, and try to fit each new phenomenon into the framework they already have. The second was that Nature has only one way of doing things, and repeats her story from time to time. The third was that things look similar because they are aspects of the same thing — fingers of the same hand.

The framework presented in this book is built under the working bet that Feynman's second and third possibilities are operative — that Nature does repeat herself, and that apparent similarities across distant domains of physics are often fingers of the same hand. The first possibility — limited imagination — is the failure mode the book is actively trying to avoid.

What this means in practice: where two mathematical descriptions of a phenomenon agree, the agreement is treated as evidence that they are looking at the same underlying structure, not as redundancy. Where a proposed connection reduces the number of independent things we have to assume, the connection is treated as worth investigating. Where a phenomenon initially looks like it needs a new mechanism, the working bet is that some viewpoint exists from which the new mechanism is unnecessary — and the task is to find that viewpoint before bolting on the mechanism. This is a working bet, not a law. Beauty — the felt sense that a description is finding its natural form — is a useful guide to which path to try first. It's not a substitute for empirical verification.

There is one further thing the reader is owed before we proceed. The framework this book describes is *strange*. Some of its central claims will resist the kind of mental picture you might naturally reach for — the rubber sheet, the expanding balloon, the surfer on the wave. We will use such pictures, because they are the cognitive scaffolding general readers have available. But the reader should know up front that the pictures are scaffolding, not the building. We climb them and leave them behind.

This is not a failure of prose. It's a feature inherited from the lineage the framework belongs to. In 1925 Heisenberg published a paper introducing what would become quantum mechanics, written in matrices of numbers rather than orbits or trajectories — and explicitly *refused* to describe what the electron was doing in the atom between transitions. Einstein, who had lit the original fire under quantum theory in 1905, found this unbearable. He spent the rest of his life trying to put the picture back in. He never quite succeeded. The picture, it turns out, was not what physics required. The mathematics was. Half a century later, Feynman opened his own popular book on quantum electrodynamics with: *"You are not going to be able to understand it. Nobody understands quantum mechanics."* He meant it literally. The strangeness was the point.

The framework's structural claims will be of this kind in places. The reasons differ — Heisenberg was responding to quantum-scale measurement disturbance; the framework here is responding to cosmological-scale geometric structure that resists picturing because there is no background in which the picture could sit. The universe is a closed three-dimensional surface whose scale is expanding — and you cannot, strictly, picture this, because picturing requires putting the surface inside a four-dimensional space the framework explicitly says doesn't exist. Time emerges from the expansion as a direction along which the manifold accumulates — and you cannot picture this either, because picturing emergence requires a background time in which the emergence is happening, which is the very thing being denied. The photon is a feature of the boundary that's doing the expanding — and pictures of "boundaries" carry implications about what's beyond them, and there is no beyond, and the picture leaks.

The book will offer pictures anyway. A surfer riding a wave. A fabric of spacetime. A wavefront expanding outward. These are useful — the human brain is wired for spatial intuition. But the pictures are aids, not the framework. Where they are imperfect, they are *honestly* imperfect, and the prose will say so. The framework's actual structural commitments are stated as commitments — sometimes through the pictures, sometimes through equations, sometimes through propositions that can be checked against measurements.

A useful discipline applies here: *what survives is what still holds when the picture you wanted stops cooperating.* The framework either describes the universe or it doesn't, and the test of that is empirical — the predictions hold or they don't, the derivations survive or they don't. Whether the framework is fully picturable in the language of phenomenal intuition is a separate question, and the answer is: not entirely. We will paint what we can, climb the pictures the human brain can hold, and let the strangeness be the strangeness where the pictures stop.

One further thing about how the book works. A move the framework makes repeatedly: what standard physics sees as two things — two particles, two events, two regions — the underlying geometry often reads more naturally as one object appearing as two. The photon's null-time will be the first instance. Entanglement is the second.

The honest warning is that this first instance is the hardest concept the book will ask you to hold. We open with it because it's the seed observation from which the rest grows; we cannot bury it in the middle of the book. You are being asked to do the hardest cognitive lifting before you have any of the framework's machinery to lean on. If the chapter resists you at first, please do not despair. Push through. It gets easier quickly. The next chapter, on entanglement, applies the same structural move to a much more concrete picture and lands considerably more directly. When you reach it, what this chapter was asking you to hold should land more easily in retrospect.

With that on the table, back to the photon.

## The Forty-Million-Year Instant

The central tension of the photon's journey is one of perspective. How can a photon take forty million years to reach us if it does not exist in time?

This is not an isolated curiosity. Special relativity already tells us that as an object's velocity approaches the speed of light, its proper time slows toward zero and its measured distances along the direction of motion shrink toward zero. The faster you move, the less time passes for you and the shorter the distances become. These are the famous time-dilation and length-contraction effects, confirmed in countless experiments since Einstein wrote them down in 1905.

The photon is the limit case of this. At exactly the speed of light, proper time stops accumulating entirely *and* proper distances contract to zero. Both — not one or the other. Two events on a photon's path are, from the photon's frame, the same event — separated by zero proper time *and* zero proper distance. This is not a mystery added to physics; it's what relativity already says, taken to its limit. Time and distance vanish together for the photon.

Let that sink in for a moment — it's the conceptual seed the rest of the chapter grows from. From the photon's frame, there is no journey: no distance to cross, no duration to wait through, no compression of cosmic scales into an instant. Emission and absorption are the same event — same place, same moment, same point. The forty million years and forty million light-years are not small, or compressed, or hidden. To the photon, they are not there.

From our perspective on Earth, of course, they are very much there. Forty million light-years out, forty million years ago, a particular star released a photon. We see a vast distance and a vast duration. We see the journey. Both readings — ours and the photon's — are correct. They are descriptions of the same underlying object, written from two different vantages.

The framework's first sharp question, then: what kind of *single* geometric object is the photon, such that one creature measuring it from outside finds forty million years and forty million light-years, while the photon itself, measured along its own existence, finds zero of both? Not "what mechanism reconciles them" — they don't need to be reconciled, because they aren't in conflict. The next section takes up the question.

A clarifying word before we go further. This is not a book about overturning relativity. Special relativity has been confirmed in every experiment that has tested it for over a hundred years, and general relativity has been confirmed in every experiment that has tested *that* for nearly as long. Every few weeks a news article appears celebrating yet another observation that "proves Einstein right." The book is not in the business of disagreeing with these results. The book is in the business of asking what the relativistic picture *means*, structurally, when you take its limit cases seriously and ask what kind of object the photon must be for both readings to hold simultaneously.

Special relativity itself already names the answer in one of its terms. In the picture the reader almost certainly carries — the fabric of spacetime, with its rubber-sheet curvature around massive bodies, its time dilation in gravitational wells, its length contraction at high speeds — massive objects trace timelike paths through the four-dimensional manifold that physics has been working with since Minkowski. The photon traces a *null path*, a path along which the metric assigns zero interval. Its worldline is what physicists call a *null geodesic* — a trajectory along which spacetime itself records no separation between start and end. This is the same fact as null-time, restated geometrically. The "null" is the structural seed: the line along which start and end register as the same point. What the framework will do, in the next section, is take this name seriously — not as a quirk of relativistic mathematics, but as a description of what the photon actually *is*, geometrically.

## Stepping Outside Time

The framework is asking the reader to do something difficult — perhaps the most difficult cognitive move physics requires. To take the photon's null-time seriously, we have to step, for a moment, out of time itself, and look at the photon's journey from a vantage where time is not something we are inside but something we are looking at.

This is not something we are built to do. We are creatures of time. Our experience is structured by time at every level — the heartbeat, the breath, the thought passing into the next thought. To imagine being outside of time is to imagine something the brain does not have a native handle for. But the photon's null-time cannot be received from inside time, because from inside time, "forty million years to cross forty million light-years" is just a fact about the photon's slow journey, and the framework's claim that this entire journey is, for the photon, zero — that the emission and the absorption are the same event — is incomprehensible. So we need to step outside.

Imagine, then, that you could stop time and look at it from the side. Not pause time so you can move around within it, but actually step away from it, far enough that time becomes a thing you can see all of at once — like looking at a map of a city rather than walking through the city. The "now" you usually experience as moving forward becomes one position on the map. Yesterday is a position behind you on the map; tomorrow is a position in front of you. The whole history of everything, from the universe's earliest moments to its most distant future, is laid out as a structure you can see in its entirety.

A physicist's version of this map is the simplest possible drawing. Fold the three spatial dimensions of the universe into one horizontal direction — pretend, for the moment, that all of space lies along a single line, even though we know it really has three dimensions — and let the vertical direction be time. Every event in the universe has a place on this map: a position in space (horizontally), at a moment in time (vertically). Your present moment is one spot. The flash that emitted a photon forty million years ago is another spot, forty million light-years to the side and forty million years below. The whole map is laid out as a single picture. From outside time, you can see all of it at once — *everything, everywhere, all at once*.

What does the photon's journey look like on this map?

Not a journey. Not a thing that happens. It looks like a line — a straight line connecting two events. At one end, the moment a star forty million light-years away emits the photon; at the other end, the moment the photon strikes the back of your eye. The line has a definite extent on the map: forty million light-years along the horizontal, forty million years along the vertical. It's not a horizontal line, and it's not a vertical line. It's a diagonal at forty-five degrees, with extent in both directions in equal measure. You can read both extents off the map directly, with a ruler.

Here a confusion needs sorting before we go further. The reader, knowing the photon experiences null-time, might try to picture the photon as not moving along the time axis at all — as a horizontal line on the map, extending only in space. This picture cannot be drawn, and the reason it cannot be drawn is the most important thing the map will tell us.

From outside time, looking at the map, the photon's line plainly has extent in both directions. The "no time" reading is real, but it's not a reading from outside the map. It's a reading from along the line itself — from the photon's own perspective, granting that "perspective" is loose here for a thing that does not experience time the way creatures of time do. And from the photon's perspective, *both* the temporal extent and the spatial extent vanish. Not just one of them. The photon does not see forty million light-years of distance and zero years of time. It sees zero of both. Emission and absorption are the same event — same place, same moment, same point. Distance and duration vanish together.

This is what the relativistic rule built into the geometry of spacetime says: for any line drawn at the photon's angle on the map, the horizontal extent and the vertical extent exactly cancel each other, and the line's internal length comes out zero. The temporal and the spatial vanish together. Picking one to keep — saying "no time, but still distance" — is not a coherent vantage. It's half a frame, and no observer is ever at half a frame.

So we have two real readings of the same single object. From outside, the line has both extents — and this is what makes our forty million years of waiting real, and what makes the forty million light-years of distance real. From the photon's vantage along the line, the geometry assigns zero to both — and this is what makes the photon's null-time and the photon's zero-distance equally real. The two readings are not in conflict. They are measurements of one geometric structure, taken from different vantages, by the rules each vantage requires.

This is the paradigm shift the framework is asking. The photon is not a thing in flight, taking time to traverse distance. The photon is a line on the map of time — a connection — and the line's two ends are aspects of one geometric structure. From outside, the structure has length in both dimensions. From along the structure, it's a single point. Both readings real; neither more fundamental than the other.

And this reframes the question the reader might naturally ask next. "How does the photon connect emission and absorption across that distance?" — the question has a different answer than intuition suggests. The photon does not connect them by crossing the distance. The photon *is* the connection. The two events are the two ends of the same geometric object. From the photon's vantage, they were never separate to begin with. From our vantage, they look separated because we are reading the line from outside, where the line has length, and so the events that anchor it appear apart. The distance and the duration are not gaps the photon must traverse; they are how the connection appears to a vantage outside of it.

We can return to being inside time now. The point of stepping outside was not to live there — we cannot — but to see, for a moment, what the photon is. What we have seen, looking from outside, is the shape of time: a geometry with structure independent of being lived through, on which the photon's journey is a single static line and our own experience of time is a path moving upward through the map. The rest of the chapter can now use this picture without re-explaining it. The photon is the line. The line is one geometric object, real from both vantages, whose two readings depend on where you measure from.

## Time is Emergent from Expansion

Now the structural question. We have seen what the photon is, geometrically — a line on the map of time, a single object whose two readings depend on where you measure from. But what about the map itself? Where does the fabric-of-spacetime come from?

The standard treatment in general relativity treats the fabric as fundamental — it's the arena in which physics happens, with its curvature responding to matter and energy through Einstein's equations. There is no obligation to treat the fabric this way. We can ask what it is *built out of*, and the answer the framework offers is structural and specific.

The fabric-of-spacetime, in the framework, is a feature of a more basic geometric process: the expansion of a closed three-dimensional space. Imagine the universe not as a four-dimensional fabric but as the *surface* of a three-dimensional expansion — a closed 3D manifold whose scale is steadily increasing. The "time direction" of the fabric emerges from this expansion. As the manifold expands, massive observers — you, me, the atoms that compose us, the clocks we build — accumulate proper time as a measure of how much the expansion has unfolded. The fabric of spacetime is the geometric record of this accumulation, with the *t*-direction being the direction along which the manifold expands.

This is not a small change at the foundations, but it does not contradict relativity at the level of any prediction relativity makes. Every test that has ever confirmed Einstein continues to confirm the framework, because the framework reproduces relativity exactly in the regime relativity has been tested in. What changes is what the relativistic structure *is*, geometrically. The fabric is no longer fundamental; it's what the expansion looks like to massive observers who are accumulating *t* as the expansion proceeds.

Now the photon. If the fabric of spacetime is a feature of the manifold's expansion, and the *t*-direction emerges from that expansion, then a particle that does not accumulate *t* must be a particle that does not engage with the expansion in the way massive particles do. The photon, with zero proper time accumulated along any of its journeys, is precisely such a particle. Geometrically, the photon is on the *boundary* of the expansion — on the closed 3D surface itself, riding the expansion as it occurs, rather than being carried along behind it. The rate at which the photon moves is the rate at which the boundary expands, which is the speed of light. The two are identical because they are the same fact: the photon's velocity is the manifold's expansion rate.

This is the same picture the previous section drew, looked at structurally instead of from outside time. The line on the map — the photon as a connection between emission and absorption — is, structurally, the photon riding the boundary of the expanding manifold. And this is where the forty million light-years come from. The photon does not cross the distance; the manifold has unfolded by this much while the photon stayed on its boundary. The endpoints have been drawn apart by the expansion. The photon-line is what records that they remain, geometrically, one object despite the unfolding.

This is the structural reading. The reader's existing picture of the photon — a particle moving at *c*, on a null geodesic, experiencing no time — is preserved exactly. What changes is what those facts *mean*. The null geodesic isn't a special trajectory through pre-existing spacetime; it's the trajectory of a particle that is on the surface that's doing the expanding. The speed of light isn't a property of the particle; it's the rate of the expansion that produces the *t*-direction every other particle moves through. And the distance isn't a distance the photon crosses; it's the amount the manifold has unfolded between the photon's two ends.

## Time, As the Expansion Unfolds

The framework rests on one equation that does most of the work. It's simple to state and we will spend the rest of the book unpacking what it implies.

H = β_NTE|Φ_NTE|²

Here, *H* is the Hubble parameter — the rate at which the universe is expanding. The reader has met this in popular cosmology, where it appears as the constant that quantifies how fast distant galaxies are receding from us. The framework adopts the standard meaning. |Φ_NTE| is the *boundary expansion field* — a geometric scalar whose strength sets the rate at which the universe expands. *β_NTE* is a coupling constant whose value characterises our universe, the way the gravitational constant or the speed of light characterises ours.

A word about |Φ_NTE| is needed here, because the notation can mislead. The reader has likely heard of the Higgs field — the field that gives mass to electrons and quarks in the Standard Model, the field whose particle was discovered at CERN in 2012. |Φ_NTE| is *not* that field. The Standard Model Higgs continues to do everything the textbook says it does, unchanged: it gives mass to electrons and quarks, and sits at its constant value of 246 GeV everywhere in the universe today. What the framework adds is one further scalar — a geometric one, sourcing the expansion of the manifold through the equation above — that does *not* give mass to particles and *can* vary across cosmic history. Two distinct fields, doing distinct work. The framework calls the new one |Φ_NTE| (the subscript stands for *null-time extension*) to keep the distinction visible. This is the "one new thing" the framework adds; everything else it does is rearrange, geometrically, what physics already had.

What the equation says, and what the rest of the book turns on, is this: the rate at which the universe expands is parameterised by the strength of |Φ_NTE|. Where the field is strong, the expansion proceeds at one rate. Where it's weak — for instance, in the gravitational well of a massive object — the expansion proceeds at a different rate. The relationship between expansion and the field's strength is fixed by the equation; the boundary expansion field is what governs how the expansion unfolds.

And because the *t*-direction emerges from the expansion, what the equation is also saying is that the rate at which proper time accumulates is parameterised by |Φ_NTE|. This is not the boundary expansion field *generating* time, in any literal sense — the framework is careful about this. The expansion is the geometric process; |Φ_NTE| is what sets the rate at which that process produces *t* for massive observers. Strong field, fast accumulation of *t*. Weak field, slow accumulation. The framework's proposal: time dilates near massive bodies because the boundary expansion field is locally weakened there, and so less *t* accumulates per unit of expansion in the well than in flat space far from any mass. There is a tighter way to put this that the framework asks the reader to hold. The reader's existing picture has spacetime curvature near the mass; the framework's picture has a weakened |Φ_NTE| at the same location. These are not two separate geometric facts that respond to each other through some additional mechanism — they are the same geometric feature, named in two different ways. |Φ_NTE| does not *respond* to curvature; it *is* curvature, viewed through the parameter that ties the local geometry to the local rate of *t*-accumulation. What the equation pins down is that relationship.

For the reader who has met gravitational time dilation in the standard treatment — clocks running slow at the bottom of a well, GPS satellites needing relativistic corrections — the framework is not contradicting any of that. Whether the framework continues to agree with general relativity in untested regimes (very strong fields, very early universe) is a question the framework's structural reading actually has things to say about, but those things are for later chapters. What changes is the structural account of *why* clocks run slow near massive bodies. The standard treatment says the geometry is curved; clocks register the curvature. The framework says the boundary expansion field is suppressed; the rate of *t*-accumulation is reduced; clocks register the reduced rate. The two accounts agree on every solar-system test, every GPS correction, and every precision measurement of gravitational redshift, and are structurally distinct in what they take to be fundamental.

The mass-giving role of the Higgs and the cosmic-expansion role of |Φ_NTE| are correlated through their shared origin at the gauge birth — what later chapters develop. For now, what the reader needs is the distinction: two scalars, two roles, coincident in their numerical value at our cosmic moment, distinct in what they do.

Now we can return to the photon and the puzzle the chapter opened with.

## The Forty-Million-Year Instant, Revisited

The chapter opened with a puzzle. The photon emitted by a star forty million light-years away has reached your eye. From its frame, no time has passed. From your frame, forty million years' worth of cosmic expansion has occurred between the emission and your absorption of it. How can both be true?

We can now answer this cleanly. The photon is the connection between emission and absorption — a single geometric object on the boundary of the expanding manifold. Both readings of it are real, taken from two vantages. From outside, the connection has length in time and space, because the manifold has unfolded between its two ends. From along the connection, both extents vanish together, because the photon's frame does not register the expansion at all. The puzzle dissolves not by reconciling two facts that looked in tension, but by recognising they were never two facts — they were two readings of one object.

The structural reason for the asymmetry, in the framework's language: massive matter — atoms, you, the clocks you build — engages with the boundary expansion field as the manifold expands, and that engagement is what registers as proper time accumulating. The forty million years is what you have accumulated while the manifold has been expanding around you. The photon does not engage with the boundary expansion field at all. Its zero is the amount of *t* it has accumulated while doing the same thing. Both numbers describe the same expansion of the same manifold. The difference is the engagement — or in the photon's case, the absence of one.

That resolution raises a question to sit with. If the photon is co-moving with the manifold's expansion at *c*, and the manifold's expansion advances in every direction at *c*, how does a particular photon have a definite direction — from a star to your eye, rather than to someone else's eye somewhere else?

The answer is that the photon's path through the manifold has two components, working in concert. One is the co-moving piece: the photon is carried with the wavefront's expansion at *c*. The other is the directional piece: along the wavefront's surface, the photon's path traces a particular curve from the emission event to the absorption event. These are not separate motions. They are two structural aspects of the same path through the manifold.

A surfer riding a wave makes the picture concrete for a moment. The wave moves toward shore at the wave's speed; the surfer is carried with it. But the surfer also moves diagonally across the wave's face, traversing the wavefront from one side to another while being carried forward. The two motions happen at once and are aspects of the surfer's single trajectory across the water. A photon's path is the same kind of compound trajectory: carried at *c* by the manifold's expansion, traversing along the wavefront from where it was emitted to where it's absorbed. Different photons trace different compound trajectories; that is what makes their directions different. The geometry of the manifold supports all of them at once, without any of them needing to be picked out by a global *now*.

Every photon you have ever observed — sunlight on your face, the glow of a screen, the dim light of distant galaxies — is a compound trajectory of this kind in the manifold. We see the universe by virtue of these connections; we are immersed in a constant flux of them.

Not a particle moving through pre-existing spacetime, but a connection in a manifold that is doing the expanding: that is the photon's structural identity. Spacetime is what the expansion looks like to massive observers, and the photon is the limit case at which there is no observer, no proper time accumulated, only the geometric connection between two events that the expansion has, in unfolding, drawn together.

## Where This Leads

The chapter has done its structural work. The framework's central commitments are in place: the universe is a closed 3D manifold whose scale is expanding; *t* emerges from this expansion as a direction along which it accumulates; the boundary expansion field |Φ_NTE| parameterises the rate via *H = β_NTE|Φ_NTE|²*; engagement with this field is what determines how strongly each particle registers the expansion as proper time, and therefore how much *t* the particle accumulates; the photon, with zero engagement, is the structural special case at which no *t* accumulates at all — the geometric connection between events the manifold's expansion has drawn apart, real from both vantages, one object with two readings.

The next chapter takes up entanglement — the second great puzzle of modern physics that the framework's structural reading dissolves rather than postulates. It's also the second instance of the same structural move the chapter has just executed: where the photon's null-time was one geometric object with two events at its ends, entanglement is one geometric object with two particles at its ends. The chapter after that develops the geometric picture of the closed 3D manifold with a careful pedagogical analogy that will be used through the rest of the book.

From there the book works out what the boundary expansion field is and where its values come from, what quantum mechanics looks like on the manifold's surface, what happened at the moment in cosmic history when the Standard Model gauge structure emerged, and what the neutrino's unresolved status means for the framework's bilateral structure.

A chapter in the middle of the book then makes the framework's first sharp empirical commitment. The framework's structural account of dark matter produces a ratio whose value is calculated, not fitted, and which can be compared directly to what the Planck satellite measures. The framework's number is κ = 5.50. Planck's measurement gives 5.38, with about 0.15 of uncertainty. The agreement at present is within a fraction of one standard deviation; tighter data will either confirm the prediction or break it. The framework lives or dies on numbers of this kind.

The chapters after that explore the consequences of the structural picture — where dark matter sits in the manifold's geometry, what the universe sounds like when read as a single coherent geometric object, and where the cosmological constant's strange smallness comes from. A chapter on the mathematical company the framework keeps lays out the structural rhymes between the framework and some of the deepest currents of mathematical physics.

The book then closes with two chapters that bring the picture and the predictions together. The first catalogues the full list of the framework's empirical commitments — the κ ratio is one of roughly fourteen, and the book is asked to be assessed against the lot. The final chapter states the working bet: what it would mean if the framework holds, and what it would mean if it does not.

Each chapter inherits the structural picture established here. The framework's claims are interdependent; introducing them serially would mean each sat incomplete until the others arrived. By loading them up front, the book can spend the rest of its time showing how each piece does its specific work, rather than rebuilding the picture each time.

The shape of time, in this framework, is the shape of the expansion. The boundary expansion field sets the rate. Massive particles accumulate *t* as the expansion proceeds. The photon does not. The photon is the connection between the events the expansion draws apart — and that is what null-time is, geometrically. **Was** is written in the geometry the expansion has already traced; **next** is what has not yet unfolded — because the manifold's expansion is the record of what has occurred, and the geometry remembers.
