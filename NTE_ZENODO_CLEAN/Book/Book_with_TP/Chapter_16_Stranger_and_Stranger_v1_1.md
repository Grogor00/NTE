## §1 Testing the Limits

We went looking for tests that should have broken the picture.

They didn't.

That is not the same sentence as: the framework predicted all of them. It did not. Some of the tests in this chapter were performed long before the framework existed. Some belong to standard quantum mechanics so deeply that any account of nature which could not absorb them would not be an alternative account at all; it would simply be wrong. A theory does not deserve a medal for surviving the double slit, Bell tests, or Berry phase. It deserves, at most, permission to keep speaking.

But permission matters. The previous chapters built a geometric story whose claims are large enough to deserve suspicion: photons at the null-time boundary; entanglement as one object with two surface intersections; quantum phase as geometry on the three-sphere; gauge birth as the moment the universe's symmetries locked; the neutrino as an unresolved species straddling the manifold's bilateral thickness; dark matter as cross-thickness gravity; the cosmological constant as the silence produced by a volume constraint. If that story is wrong in a simple way, the strange experiments of twentieth- and twenty-first-century physics are where it ought to show.

The reason is straightforward. These experiments are not peripheral. They are the places where the ordinary language of particles moving through space and time starts to tear. A neutron rotated through 360 degrees does not quite come back to itself. A charged particle can feel the presence of a magnetic field in a region it never enters. Entangled systems violate the limits any local hidden-variable story can obey. A photon can be asked, after the fact, what kind of history it had. Driven quantum matter can form order in time rather than only in space. Gravity itself may be put on trial in a tabletop experiment designed to ask whether two masses can become entangled through their mutual gravitational field.

These are not decorative oddities. They are pressure points. Each one asks whether the framework's favourite words - boundary, phase, projection, surface, geometry, null-time, bilateral support - are explanations or only poetry.

The honest result, so far, is more modest and more interesting than triumph. The framework did not stumble where it was expected to stumble. It did not need to deny the experiments. It did not need to add emergency exceptions. In several cases the experiments looked, after translation, almost uncomfortably natural. The picture had room where standard intuition has trouble finding room.

This chapter walks through six such cases. Five are retrospective: the world has already performed the test, and the framework has to show that it can absorb the result without cheating. The sixth is a forward commitment: an experiment not yet complete, where the framework has something real at stake.

The point is not to declare victory. Passing old tests is not the same as making new predictions. The point is to find the boundary of the picture by pushing on it. If the picture is fragile, these are the places where it should crack. If it keeps holding, then Chapter 15's scorecard begins to look less like a list of lucky hits and more like the empirical surface of a single geometry.

## Case A: The Neutron That Needs Two Turns

Start with an object you can imagine in your hand: a small arrow.

Turn the arrow through a full circle. After 360 degrees it points the way it did at the beginning. A second turn does nothing new. This is how ordinary objects behave, and it is how our visual imagination expects rotation to work.

A neutron is not an ordinary object in that sense. It has spin one-half, which means its quantum state has a peculiar rotational character. Rotate the apparatus that describes the neutron's spin through 360 degrees, and the neutron's wavefunction does not return to exactly the same state. It returns with its sign reversed. Only after a second full turn, 720 degrees in total, does it come back to itself.

This sounds like a mathematical trick until an interferometer makes it visible. Split a neutron beam into two paths. Rotate the spin state along one path and not the other. Recombine the paths. If a 360-degree rotation truly changed nothing, the interference pattern would say so. It does not. The sign reversal changes the relative phase between the two paths, and the apparatus records it. The neutron really does carry the two-turn character.

The first pedagogical hurdle is that the neutron has not become a tiny arrow that secretly points the wrong way after one turn. Spin is not literal spinning. The neutron is not a ball rotating on an axle. Spin is a feature of the state space in which the neutron's quantum state lives. For ordinary spatial rotations, a circle is enough to bring the object back. For spinors, the state space has a double covering: the physical orientation we draw in space is only the shadow of a deeper object whose full return requires two loops.

That phrase, "double covering", is where the framework should either become useful or collapse into jargon. In the framework's language, the quantum state is not a little property carried by a particle through an already finished space. It is a geometric configuration on the state space associated with the boundary. Chapter 6 treated the three-sphere, S³, as the natural home of quantum phase before the Hopf projection reduces it to the two-sphere of observable states. A spinor's strange two-turn behaviour is exactly the kind of thing one expects when the visible orientation is a projected reading of a richer geometry.

The neutron experiment is therefore a retrospective survival test. The framework did not predict the original neutron interferometry result; quantum mechanics did. But the result is friendly to the framework's ontology in a specific way. It says that the sign of a quantum amplitude is not disposable bookkeeping. It says that a loop in the underlying state space can have a physical effect even when the surface picture looks as if it has returned to where it started. It says phase is geometry with consequences.

That is the same discipline the framework has already needed elsewhere. The Hopf fibration is built around the fact that phase is not simply visible position. The photon chapter used null-time geometry to explain why two surface events can belong to one underlying structure. The entanglement chapter used the rod-through-the-ball picture to separate what the surface sees from what the object is. The neutron's 4π rotation adds another version of the same lesson: the surface may say "one turn"; the underlying state space may say "not yet".

There is a caution here. Standard quantum mechanics handles this perfectly. The framework gains no special credit for reproducing a fact already built into spinor mathematics. The only claim worth making is narrower. If the framework had treated particles as ordinary little objects moving through ordinary space with quantum phase added later as a label, the neutron would have exposed it. It does not. The neutron's two-turn return belongs naturally to a geometry-first account.

That is a pass, but a retrospective one. The experiment was not waiting for NTE. NTE was waiting to be judged by it.

## Case B: Phase Without a Local Field

The Aharonov-Bohm effect is one of the cleanest ways physics has found to embarrass common sense.

Set up a long solenoid, a coil that confines a magnetic field inside itself. Arrange things so that charged particles, usually electrons, travel along two paths around the solenoid but never pass through the region where the magnetic field is nonzero. Classically, nothing should happen. The electron only experiences a force where there is an electric or magnetic field. If the field is absent everywhere along the electron's path, the electron should have no way to know the solenoid is there.

But the interference pattern shifts.

The electrons do not feel a local magnetic force. They still acquire a phase that depends on the magnetic flux enclosed by their paths. The magnetic field is hidden inside the solenoid; the phase lives in the geometry around it. The experiment says, bluntly, that the gauge potential is not merely a calculational convenience. Something about the global structure of the gauge field matters even where the local field strength vanishes.

Berry phase broadens the same lesson. Carry a quantum system slowly around a closed loop in its space of possible configurations. When it returns to the starting configuration, its state can have acquired an extra phase depending only on the path taken through that space. Not on how fast it moved, not on the local energy tick by tick, but on the geometry of the loop. The system comes back to the same visible setting with a memory of the route.

For the reader, the important distinction is between force and geometry. A force pushes locally. It acts here, now, on the object passing through this place. A geometric phase is different. It is more like walking around a mountain and returning to your starting point with your compass subtly reoriented because the surface you walked on was curved. Nothing shoved the compass at one special point. The path as a whole mattered.

This is dangerous territory for any framework that wants to rebuild physics from a geometric boundary. If the framework's geometry cannot carry gauge information globally, it fails. Gauge fields are not optional decoration in the Standard Model. They are the structure of electromagnetism, the weak force, and the strong force. Aharonov-Bohm and Berry phase are the experiments that say: do not treat gauge structure as a local field pasted onto space after the fact. It has global teeth.

NTE's survival here comes from the role of the Hopf structure and gauge birth. In the framework's account, quantum phase begins as geometry on S³. The U(1) fibre is not an arbitrary mathematical dial; it is the circular phase direction whose quotient gives the visible two-sphere of states. Gauge birth is the locking event that turns pre-temporal symmetry into the gauge structure of the Standard Model. If that account is even approximately right, then phase acquired around a loop should not be surprising. Loops in the underlying fibre geometry should matter.

The Aharonov-Bohm effect then reads as a surface version of a deeper fact: the particle's local path does not exhaust the physical situation. The enclosed gauge geometry contributes to the phase relation between the two paths, and interference can reveal that contribution even when no local force acts along either path. Berry phase says the same thing in a wider language: a state transported through configuration space carries holonomy, the geometric record of how the loop sits in the space.

That word can be translated. Holonomy is what happens when "coming back to the same point" is not the same as "coming back unchanged". A vector carried around a curved surface can return rotated. A quantum state carried around a loop can return with a phase. A neutron spinor rotated once can return with a changed sign. The family resemblance is not accidental. It is the mathematics of geometry refusing to be reduced to local position.

Again, this is not a new prediction by NTE. These effects are established quantum physics. The framework's test is whether it can house them without special pleading. It can, and more than that, it appears to need the same kind of structure for its own internal reasons. Gauge geometry without local field is not an embarrassment for a boundary-and-phase account. It is almost a warning label on the door: if you want to explain quantum mechanics, be prepared for global geometry to count.

The Aharonov-Bohm and Berry cases therefore push the framework in exactly the right place. They ask whether "phase as geometry" is a slogan or a working discipline. The answer, retrospectively, is that the slogan points in the right direction. The loop remembers because the geometry is real.

## Case C: Bell, Hardy, and the Surface of One Object

Bell tests are the old fire.

Prepare two particles together. Send them far apart. Choose measurement settings independently at the two detectors. Record the outcomes. Repeat the experiment many times. If each particle carried pre-written answers to every possible question, and if no signal travelled faster than light between the detectors, the correlations must obey Bell's inequality. Experiments violate it.

That sentence has acquired so much philosophical weight that it is worth slowing down. Bell's theorem does not say merely that quantum mechanics is strange. It says that a particular kind of explanation cannot work. The particles cannot be little sealed envelopes carrying all their answers from the source. The correlations are too strong for that. At the same time, the experiments do not allow usable faster-than-light signalling. One detector cannot send a message to the other by choosing its setting. Relativity survives, but the envelope picture dies.

Hardy's paradox sharpens the discomfort. It gives a version of quantum nonlocality without relying on inequalities, using a set of possible and impossible outcomes that cannot all be assigned consistently if the particles had definite local properties before measurement. The details are more technical than a chapter like this needs, but the moral is plain: quantum systems resist being described as collections of separately completed local facts waiting to be read.

Chapter 2 gave the framework's answer in its simplest picture: one rod, two surface intersections. The two laboratory events are real. The detectors are local. The outcomes are recorded at different places on the surface. But the underlying object being intersected is one. The correlations are not messages passed across space; they are the shape of the object whose two intersections are being read.

Bell and Hardy test that picture directly. If the framework were a hidden-variable theory in disguise, it would fail here. Hidden-variable theories of the local envelope kind are exactly what Bell rules out. NTE's claim is not that the particles secretly carry more information. It is that the word "particles" has been attached to the wrong level of the description. The two registered events are two surface readings of a single underlying geometric structure.

This is why the language of surface ontology matters. The surface is not an illusion. We live on it. Our detectors occupy it. The click in one laboratory and the click in another laboratory are separate events in the post-temporal manifold. Their separation is real for every ordinary causal process. Nothing in the framework permits a radio message, a force, or an instruction to race between them faster than light.

But the correlation is not an ordinary causal process moving through the surface. It belongs to the underlying object that meets the surface twice. If the object is one, then the correlation need not be manufactured after the first measurement. It is not assembled by communication. It is disclosed by the pair of measurements.

That distinction is the only way the framework survives Bell honestly. It must preserve locality for signals while denying separability for the entangled object. Standard quantum mechanics also does this, though often in a language that leaves the ontology deliberately thin. NTE makes the ontology thick: the entangled pair is one geometric object with two surface intersections.

Hardy's version helps because it removes a common escape hatch. It is tempting to imagine that the strange correlations are only statistical, somehow a property of large ensembles rather than of individual cases. Hardy's setup makes the contradiction feel sharper at the level of possible events. The framework's response is the same: the attempt to assign separate local facts to the two apparent particles is the wrong move. The two apparent particles are not the fundamental inventory.

The retrospective pass here is strong but not distinguishing in the way Chapter 15's quantitative predictions are distinguishing. Quantum mechanics already predicts Bell violations. The framework does not win by saying "Bell happens." It only remains viable by explaining why Bell is not fatal to its own geometry. It passes because the geometry from Chapter 2 was already built to treat entanglement as one object rather than two.

There is even a small positive sign. The Tsirelson ceiling, the maximum quantum violation at 2√2, is not arbitrary in the framework's reading. It is the angular limit produced by the state-space geometry. That does not separate NTE from quantum mechanics empirically, because quantum mechanics also gives the same ceiling. But it does mean the framework is not merely accommodating the existence of Bell violations. It is reading their precise boundedness as a property of the geometry it already uses.

That is the right level of claim. Bell and Hardy do not prove NTE. They do something more modest and more necessary: they fail to kill it at the point where many realist pictures die.

## Case D: Two Slits in Time

The ordinary double slit is the experiment everyone learns first because it is the simplest way to make quantum strangeness visible. Send light, electrons, or other quantum objects toward two narrow openings. If no measurement reveals which slit each object passed through, an interference pattern appears. If the path is measured, the interference disappears. The lesson is not that the particle literally becomes a tiny wave in ordinary space. The lesson is that alternatives can interfere when the experimental setup preserves their phase relation.

The time-domain version moves the puzzle from space into time.

Instead of two spatial slits, the experiment creates two temporal openings: two brief moments at which a system can pass, scatter, or be emitted. The alternatives are not "left slit" and "right slit". They are "earlier time window" and "later time window". If the two alternatives remain coherent, the interference appears in energy or frequency rather than in position on a screen. Time itself has become the axis along which the slits are arranged.

Delayed-choice experiments push the discomfort further. In Wheeler's famous version, the choice of whether to reveal path information or preserve interference can be made after the quantum system has already entered the apparatus. Popular accounts sometimes make this sound as if the future reaches backward and rewrites the past. That is the wrong reading. The cleaner lesson is that the experiment's final arrangement determines which question can be meaningfully asked of the whole quantum process. "Which path did it really take?" is not always a valid question. Sometimes the apparatus was arranged to measure a phase relation between alternatives, not a completed history through one alternative.

For NTE, these experiments are dangerous because the framework has said unusually strong things about time. It has not treated time as a universal river in which all physics happens. It has treated time as a post-locking structure, a feature of how the boundary unfolds and how massive species accumulate proper time after gauge birth. Photons sit at the null-time edge. Entanglement belongs to a geometry not exhausted by surface time. The framework therefore has to be very careful when experiments put time into the double-slit apparatus itself.

The survival is cleaner than one might expect. Time-domain interference says that temporal alternatives can play the same phase role spatial alternatives play. That is exactly what a geometry-first view of phase should allow. If a quantum process is not a little bead moving through a pre-written sequence of instants, then two possible temporal openings can remain coherent until the apparatus forces a particular kind of projection. The interference is not a contradiction of time. It is a sign that "time of passage" was not a settled local fact in the way classical intuition wants it to be.

Delayed choice adds the same lesson from another angle. The framework does not need the future to change the past. It only needs to deny that there was a single completed surface history of the relevant kind before the measurement context made such a history meaningful. That denial is already built into the earlier chapters. The photon does not accumulate proper time along its own path. The entangled object is not two completed local particles carrying independent facts. The neutrino, before detection, is not one flavour in the way a marble is one colour. Quantum alternatives remain geometric possibilities until an interaction projects them into the surface record.

The useful analogy is a book with transparent pages. If several pages are stacked and you look through them from above, a mark on page three and a mark on page seven may overlap into one visible shape. Asking which page "made" the shape before choosing a focus setting is not always a meaningful question. Change the focus and the page structure resolves differently. Nothing has travelled backward in time. The optical arrangement determines what aspect of the layered object is being read.

Time-domain slits make the layered object temporal. They ask whether the framework can let phase coherence span alternatives separated not only in place but in time. Because the framework treats time as part of the emergent surface order rather than the deepest container, the answer is yes. The temporal separation of the alternatives is real in the laboratory description. The coherence between them belongs to the underlying quantum geometry, which is not forced to have chosen one completed classical history before detection.

There is a scope limit. These experiments do not test NTE's gauge-birth scale. They do not reach the regime where time itself fails to have post-temporal meaning. The energies involved are ordinary laboratory energies, fantastically far from the Planckian edge where the framework says temporal structure crystallised. So the time-domain double slit is not a direct falsifier of the deepest claim. It is a compatibility test of the language: can the framework speak coherently when interference is arranged in time rather than space?

It can. More than that, it sounds less strained than a picture that insists on completed histories first and quantum interference second. If time is part of the shape, then a slit cut in time is still a slit cut in geometry.

## Case E: Time Crystals and Rondeau Order

The phrase "time crystal" sounds like it escaped from fantasy, but the physics is precise.

A spatial crystal breaks the continuous symmetry of space. Empty space looks the same if you slide your coordinate system a little to the left or right. A crystal does not. Its atoms sit in a repeating pattern, so shifting by one lattice spacing brings the pattern back, while shifting by half a spacing does not. The symmetry has been reduced from continuous translation to discrete repetition.

Time crystals ask whether something analogous can happen in time. Can a many-body quantum system settle into a pattern that repeats in time in a way not simply identical to the external rhythm driving it? In thermal equilibrium, the answer is no. Equilibrium systems cannot keep ticking forever in their ground state without paying energy bookkeeping debts. But driven, non-equilibrium systems are different. Under periodic driving, especially in Floquet settings, systems can respond at a sub-harmonic of the drive: push once per cycle, and the system's observable repeats only every two cycles. The response has a rhythm of its own.

The lay picture is a strobe light and a spinning object. Flash the strobe at the drive rhythm. A normal driven system looks the same every flash because it is following the drive. A discrete time crystal can look as if it alternates: first flash, one face; second flash, the opposite face; third flash, back to the first. The system is not merely wiggling. It has locked into a stable sub-harmonic pattern.

Chapter 8 used a strobe analogy for the neutrino's chirality sampling. The unresolved neutrino's underlying configuration was like a spinning coin, with our orientation's weak interaction catching one phase and the mirror orientation catching the opposite phase. Time crystals are not neutrinos, and the analogy should not be overextended. But the same pedagogical shape is useful: a system can have an underlying rhythm, and what the apparatus samples at special moments can reveal an order not visible in the ordinary continuous-time blur.

The 2025 observation of a time rondeau crystal makes the test stranger. Rondeau order combines long-time stroboscopic order with short-time disorder. In the musical form, a repeating theme returns between contrasting passages. In the experiment, using carbon-13 nuclear spins in diamond as a quantum simulator, the system maintained a stable long-time pattern at special stroboscopic points while the micromotion between those points could be tunably disordered. Look only at the right repeated moments and the order is there. Look between them and the motion need not be simply periodic. The order is not the absence of disorder; it coexists with disorder on a different temporal scale.

For NTE, this was an alarmingly easy pass.

It should not have been, at least by naive expectation. The framework has a lot invested in the difference between underlying phase structure and what a post-temporal apparatus samples. It says the observed world is a projected reading of deeper geometry. It says the neutrino's handedness asymmetry is phase sampling. It says the gauge-birth transition locks pre-temporal structure into the rhythms and symmetries our side of the manifold can observe. If real quantum matter had refused to support robust temporal order away from equilibrium, or if sub-harmonic response had turned out to be a fragile artefact with no stable phase character, the framework would have lost one of its favourite analogies.

Instead, the laboratory found a zoo of temporal order. Period-doubled response. Prethermal stability. Structured drives. Quasiperiodic and random components. Rondeau order, where the stroboscopic theme survives while the short-time motion varies. None of this proves the framework. Standard non-equilibrium quantum many-body physics is the proper home of these experiments. The point is simpler: nature is comfortable with order in time that is not reducible to a smooth little clock.

The KMS phrase belongs here, though gently. In equilibrium statistical physics, the Kubo-Martin-Schwinger condition is one of the mathematical ways thermal equilibrium knows about time. It encodes a precise periodicity property in imaginary time, a deep relation between temperature and temporal structure. Time crystals live outside that equilibrium comfort. They are driven, non-equilibrium phases. They do not say that time is unreal. They say that temporal order is richer when the system is not forced into equilibrium's narrow grammar.

That matters for the framework because NTE's deepest claims sit at the edge of equilibrium intuition. Gauge birth is not a little event inside an already thermal universe. It is the transition through which the post-temporal rules become the rules. A framework built around that transition should not be surprised that temporal order in quantum matter depends on driving, projection, stroboscopic sampling, and phase locking. It should expect the language of time to become structural before it becomes ordinary.

The rondeau case is especially helpful because it refuses the simplest story. It is not merely a clock that ticks. It is a pattern where order and disorder occupy different levels of the temporal description. That is close in spirit to the framework's repeated distinction between surface record and underlying object. The surface may record disorder between the strobes while the stroboscopic structure remains ordered. The reader has seen versions of this before: two surface intersections, one rod; local no-field paths, global gauge phase; a neutron apparently back after one turn, spinor not yet returned.

The pass is "alarmingly easy" because the experiment did not ask NTE to contort itself. It asked whether time can be a medium of geometric order, phase sampling, and non-equilibrium structure. The framework's answer was already yes.

That does not make time crystals a prediction of NTE. They are not. The chapter's discipline forbids pretending otherwise. They are a compatibility test with a strong pedagogical echo. The echo is useful because it lets a reader feel, in a modern laboratory system, why the framework keeps insisting that the observed tick is not always the whole rhythm.

## Case F: BMV as a Forward Commitment

The previous cases look backward. The world performed the experiments; the framework arrived later and had to see whether it could survive them. BMV is different.

BMV names a family of proposed experiments, associated with Sougato Bose, Anupam Mazumdar, and collaborators in one line and with Chiara Marletto and Vlatko Vedral in another, designed to test whether gravity can mediate entanglement. The setup, in broad outline, is simple to say and extremely hard to build. Put small masses into quantum superpositions of position. Let them interact gravitationally while shielded from other forces as carefully as possible. Recombine the states. If the two masses become entangled, and if all non-gravitational channels have been excluded, the natural conclusion is that the gravitational field itself carried quantum information.

That would be a profound result. Not because it would complete quantum gravity, but because it would rule out a large class of evasions. If two quantum systems become entangled only through gravity, then gravity is not merely a classical background field responding to expectation values. It has, at least in that channel, the capacity to participate in quantum entanglement.

For standard physics, BMV is a test of whether gravity has to be quantum in a minimal operational sense. For NTE, it is more specific. The framework's gravity sector is not a detachable add-on. Cross-thickness gravity, the Janus structure, the unresolved neutrino bridge, and the cosmological predictions in Chapter 15 all sit on a geometry in which curvature and quantum structure are not separate provinces joined by treaty after the fact. If gravity could not mediate entanglement between quantum masses, NTE would have a serious problem.

So the framework expects a positive BMV outcome.

That sentence should be read carefully. It is not a calculation of the experimental signal strength in a particular apparatus. The detailed matter-coupling Lagrangian needed for a quantitative correction has not been closed in the lay account. Nor is the framework claiming that standard quantum gravity cannot also expect a positive result. Many physicists already regard a positive BMV result as the natural expectation if gravity is quantum. NTE's commitment is not unique in sign. Its importance lies in coupling.

Chapter 15 gave the tensor-to-scalar ratio as one of the coming cosmological tests: the hilltop reading puts r near 0.003 to 0.004, within reach of CMB-S4 and LiteBIRD. That prediction and the BMV expectation are not independent ornaments. They probe the same broad graviton-mediated sector from two wildly different directions. The early universe tests the gravitational sector through primordial tensor modes in the cosmic microwave background. BMV tests it in the laboratory through mesoscopic masses trying to entangle across a gravitational interaction.

This creates a two-channel risk. If r lands in the predicted window and BMV is positive, the gravitational sector receives support from both cosmology and the laboratory. If r fails badly but BMV is positive, then the laboratory says gravity can carry quantum entanglement while the framework's inflationary reading has missed the tensor amplitude. If r looks right but BMV is negative under clean experimental conditions, the framework faces a different tension: the early-universe graviton sector appears right while the laboratory channel refuses the quantum-gravity implication. If both fail, the sector has to be rebuilt.

That is what a forward commitment should look like. Not a vague statement that any result can be absorbed, but a surface on which different outcomes cost different things.

BMV also brings the chapter's theme back to the boundary. Entanglement in Chapter 2 was one underlying object with two surface intersections. Gravity in the later chapters is the geometry of the manifold's shape and thickness. BMV asks whether geometry can itself be the mediator that turns two separate quantum systems into one entangled structure. If the answer is yes, the experiment will have made a tabletop version of the book's central instinct: quantum connection and geometry belong together.

If the answer is no, and the no survives all the experimental caveats, NTE does not get to shrug. A negative BMV result would not immediately falsify every part of the framework, because experimental loopholes and alternative couplings would have to be assessed carefully. But a clean, repeated, well-shielded failure where gravity should have mediated entanglement would be a real strike against the framework's gravity sector. Coupled with a failure of r near the predicted scale, it would be worse than a strike. It would say the framework has mistaken the shape of the graviton-mediated channel.

That is why BMV belongs at the end of this chapter. The earlier cases granted permission to keep speaking. BMV asks the framework to say something before the result is in.

## Toward the Score

The strange tests do not prove the theory.

That sentence should remain visible. A framework can survive old experiments because it was built broadly enough, or because its language is flexible, or because the experiments do not cut deeply enough to separate it from standard quantum mechanics. Retrospective compatibility is necessary, but it is not sufficient. The history of physics is full of beautiful pictures that could explain yesterday and predict nothing tomorrow.

What the cases in this chapter show is narrower. The framework has not fallen at the obvious fences. Spinor rotation did not break its account of phase geometry. Aharonov-Bohm and Berry phase did not embarrass its claim that gauge geometry is real beyond local field strength. Bell and Hardy did not force it into forbidden hidden variables. Time-domain double slits and delayed choice did not require a classical history to exist before the apparatus made such a history meaningful. Time crystals, including rondeau order, did not make temporal order look like a smooth equilibrium clock. BMV now stands as a forward commitment in the gravity sector rather than another retrospective pass.

That is a useful result, but it is not the result the book finally needs.

Chapter 15 is where the framework puts numbers and absence claims on the table: κ near 5.50, n_s near 0.9685, r near a few times 10^-3, no WIMP, no fourth generation, Majorana neutrinos, the 40-megaparsec coherence transition, the cosmological constant at the stated residual level, and the rest of the score. Those are the tests that can break the picture in the way a serious scientific proposal has to be breakable. The cases in this chapter prepare the ground by showing that the picture is not already dead on contact with quantum strangeness.

The mood, then, is not triumph. It is unease of the productive kind. The more directly the old strange experiments were translated into the framework's language, the less alien they looked. That can be a warning sign if the language is too loose. It can also be what happens when a viewpoint is close to the thing the experiments have been trying to show us all along.

The only honest way to tell the difference is to keep pushing.

The old tests asked whether the framework could survive the known strangeness. The next tests ask whether it can pay for its own claims. That is why this chapter belongs beside The Score rather than in place of it. Passing the boundary tests gives the framework permission to enter the arena. The score decides what happens there.
