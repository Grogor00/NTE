## 1. Chapter purpose and pedagogical role

Chapter 9 is the book's **strongest empirical hook**. The framework predicts κ = 5.50 for the dark-matter-to-baryon ratio. Planck has measured 5.38 ± 0.15. The match is 0.83σ, with **zero free parameters** in the prediction. CR identified this as the chapter the book most needs and currently doesn't have.

The chapter has three jobs:

1. **Make the prediction visible as a real test.** The reader needs to feel that the framework is staked on a specific number and could have failed. Honesty discipline: this means showing both that the framework's structural commitments narrow the prediction range *before* the calculation (κ ≥ 0, κ ≤ ∞, no other framework-internal values are accessible) and that within that range the prediction is forced to a specific value (5.50) by the closure step.

2. **Walk the reader through the derivation chain at lay register.** The math is genuinely fiddly (Hopf monopole projection on S³, topological protection of the lowest harmonic, geometric resummation under self-consistency). The chapter has to keep the structural meaning intact while shedding the technical apparatus the reader doesn't need.

3. **Be honest about what the prediction depends on.** What's load-bearing? What could break? Where is the framework still doing structural research? The 0.83σ discrepancy is small but not zero — the chapter has to say something about it without inventing positive candidates the corpus has explicitly closed.

This is the chapter that makes the framework empirically *vulnerable* in a way the rest of the book does not. The other chapters develop structural readings of phenomena that are already known. This chapter writes down a number and stakes the framework on it. That posture should be visible in the prose.

### §1 — The Empirical Mystery (~700 words)

**Beat A.** Open with the measurement. In our universe, dark matter outweighs ordinary matter by roughly 5.4 to 1. Planck's 2018 release pinned this number to 5.38 ± 0.15. It is one of the most precisely measured numbers in cosmology. It also is one of the most under-explained.

**Beat B.** The Standard Model has nothing to say about this ratio. Particle physics tells us what baryons are and how they interact; it has no candidate for what dark matter is, and no derivation of why there should be 5.4 times as much of it. Dark matter is an inference from gravitational observations — galaxy rotation curves, gravitational lensing, the cosmic microwave background — and the standard treatment infers its existence without explaining its abundance.

**Beat C.** Various proposals exist: WIMPs at specific masses, axions at specific decay constants, primordial black holes at specific abundances. Each candidate, if it exists, gives a way to *fit* κ ≈ 5.4 by choosing parameters in the candidate's theory. None of them *predicts* the number from structural inputs that were not tuned to match.

**Beat D.** The framework is going to do something different. It will predict κ = 5.50 from inputs that come from elsewhere in the framework — the Hopf fibration on S³, the unresolved neutrino's bilateral support, the framework's CPN-conjugate orientation structure — none of which were chosen to make this number come out right. The chapter walks the reader through the prediction.

**Beat E (recalibrated per CR6).** A note up front about what the framework's bilateral structure makes possible. Within the framework's setup, κ has a constrained range: it must be non-negative (a closed series with a positive coupling has no negative fixed point), and the closure series only converges if the bare coupling is less than 1 (so κ is finite). A naive mirror-matter picture within the framework would predict κ = 1 (equal sectors, equal contribution). A no-bilateral-structure picture would predict κ = 0 (no dark matter to explain). The framework's specific bilateral structure, with self-referential closure, picks out a specific value: 5.50. The match with Planck's 5.38 is the test the framework could have failed — not because κ could have been any number, but because *given the framework's structural commitments*, κ = 5.50 is forced, and the question is whether nature agrees.

**Forward signal:** the chapter is staked on a specific number whose value the framework's structure determines. The reader should feel this.

The framework's structural commitments don't pick out any preferred direction on S³, so the density must be angularly uniform. It is geometry: the area-normalisation of the unit two-sphere. The technical corpus develops this through the Hopf fibration's projection of the three-sphere down to the two-sphere via circles as fibres; the lay-register version is angular uniformity gives the area-normalisation factor.

**Beat C (reframed per AC Area 3).** Why three? The unresolved neutrino has three Majorana mass eigenmodes. **Beat D.** Multiply: κ₀ = 3 × 1/√(4π) = 3/√(4π) ≈ 0.846. This is the bare coupling — the strength of the cross-thickness coupling per unit stress-energy *before* the closure step's feedback is taken into account. It is set entirely by geometry. Three eigenmodes, each contributing the area-normalisation factor of the lowest harmonic on the two-sphere. No tunable parameters.

**Beat E (reframed per AC Area 3).** The protection footnote. In a generic field-theoretic calculation, one might worry that higher harmonics on the two-sphere contribute to κ₀ at subleading order, and that the topological argument is only an approximation. So κ₀ = 3/√(4π) is *exact* in its topological-projection step, not approximate. (This is foreshadowing for §8's "what is and isn't load-bearing" treatment: while the topological projection is exact, the derivation as a whole rests on additional structural inputs — see §8.)

**Beat F.** Rhetorical pivot: at this point, the framework has produced one number — κ₀ ≈ 0.846 — from pure geometry. This number is interesting but it is not yet κ. The closure step is what turns κ₀ into the observed κ.

### §5 — The Closure (~900 words)

*(Section unchanged from v1; no AC or CR findings.)*

**Beat A.** Recall §3's self-referential feature. The cross-thickness coupling acts on the total stress-energy in each orientation, including the bridge's own contribution coming back. This is the geometric series setup.

**Beat B.** In symbols (lightly). The total stress-energy in our orientation, T, is the sum of baryonic stress-energy T_b and the cross-thickness contribution. The cross-thickness contribution is κ₀ times the total stress-energy in the other orientation, T'. By CPN symmetry, T' = T (both orientations have the same structure under the framework's bilateral picture). So the total stress-energy in our orientation is:

T = T_b + κ₀ T

**Beat C.** Solve. T = T_b / (1 − κ₀). The dark matter contribution to our orientation is the part of T that is not T_b, i.e. the cross-thickness contribution κ₀ T = κ₀ T_b / (1 − κ₀). The dark-matter-to-baryon ratio is therefore:

κ = κ₀ / (1 − κ₀)

**Beat D.** Plug in κ₀ = 3/√(4π) ≈ 0.846. Compute κ = 0.846 / 0.154 ≈ 5.50.

**Beat E.** Why "geometric series"? Optionally develop the resummation as a perturbative picture: at first order, the cross-thickness coupling contributes κ₀ T_b. But at second order, the coupling sees its own contribution from the other orientation and contributes κ₀² T_b. At third order, κ₀³ T_b. The infinite sum κ₀ + κ₀² + κ₀³ + ... = κ₀ / (1 − κ₀) is the closed form. This is the same geometric series the reader has met in basic algebra; the chapter's job is to explain that the geometric series here is not a calculational trick but a real structural feedback.

**Beat F.** Pedagogical landing: from a coupling that geometry makes 0.846 (less than 1, so the series converges), the closure produces 5.50. The amplification factor — roughly six — is the amount by which the cross-thickness feedback enhances the bare bilateral contribution. The chapter should let the reader feel that this amplification is structural rather than fortuitous: it has the form of a geometric series, and the answer it produces is precisely what we observe.

### §7 — The 0.83σ Match (~500 words)

**Beat A.** Side-by-side. The framework's prediction: κ = 5.50. Planck's measurement: κ = 5.38 ± 0.15. The difference is 0.12, which is 0.83σ — well within statistical agreement.

CMB-S4 will settle whether the residual is a genuine puzzle for the framework or just the size of measurement noise.
