## §1 The Trousers of Time

There is a recurring conceit in Pratchett's Discworld novels of *the trousers of time*: at certain critical moments, the universe diverges into two possible histories, the way a pair of trousers diverges into two legs. You walk up one leg or the other; whichever you choose, you cannot then come down the other leg without going back to the seam. The two legs, in Pratchett's metaphor, are two different ways the same universe could go from a given decision point.

The framework of this book has, almost without meaning to, realised something close to this picture as physics. The universe is one manifold with two orientations. They are not two parallel realities, exactly — Pratchett's elves do not live in the other orientation, nor does some alternate version of the reader who took a different career. The two orientations are related by the framework's CPN symmetry, which constrains them tightly enough that they have the same matter content, the same forces, the same large-scale structure. They are mirror images in a precise mathematical sense, sharing the seam at the Janus boundary at *N* = 0 and never crossing it. We occupy one. The other is real, with its own galaxies and stars and (possibly, in some loose sense) observers, all evolving under the same physics in their own forward direction of time.

The previous chapter took this bilateral structure and used it to derive a specific number — the dark-matter-to-baryon ratio — that the framework's structural commitments force to be 5.50, in agreement with what cosmologists measure. The derivation was the chapter's empirical centrepiece. But the bilateral structure does more than produce one number. It reframes the entire dark-sector picture — galaxy rotation curves, gravitational lensing, the cosmic web, the negative results from direct-detection experiments. This chapter takes the framework's reading into that broader phenomenology.

The chapter's title is the structural claim of the framework's reading of dark matter. The matter we observe gravitationally, but not electromagnetically, is not a new species of particle in our orientation. It is the matter content of the *other trouser* — the orientation we do not occupy, whose curvature registers in our orientation through the cross-thickness coupling Chapter 9 developed. Dark matter is, in this picture, already accounted for. It is what the framework predicts must exist, given that we live on one side of a manifold that has another side.

What follows is what this picture commits us to, observationally — and where the framework either succeeds in matching what cosmologists have observed, or fails.

## §2 The Empirical Case

Before going further, the chapter needs to be honest about the empirical case for dark matter. The framework is reframing a real observed phenomenon, not constructing one. The mass that gravitates in our universe but does not emit light is not a hypothesis. It is one of the most thoroughly established facts in cosmology, supported by independent lines of evidence that all converge on the same conclusion.

The first line is *galaxy rotation curves*. In the 1970s, Vera Rubin and Kent Ford measured the rotation speeds of stars in the outer regions of spiral galaxies. The speeds did not fall off the way they should have if the only mass were the visible matter — the stars and gas the telescope could see. Instead, the rotation curves stayed flat far out beyond the visible disc, as if the galaxies were embedded in a much larger, much more massive halo of something whose mass extended well past the luminous edge. The pattern was found in galaxy after galaxy. The amount of "extra" mass, in each case, was roughly five times the visible matter.

The second line is *gravitational lensing*. Light passing near a massive object is bent, by an amount that depends on the mass. Cosmological surveys map the lensing pattern around galaxy clusters and find that the total mass inferred from the lensing far exceeds the visible matter — by roughly the same factor as the rotation-curve evidence demands.

The third line is the *cosmic microwave background*. The CMB's pattern of temperature fluctuations encodes the relative amounts of ordinary matter and dark matter at the moment the universe became transparent, around 380,000 years after the gauge birth. The acoustic peaks in the CMB power spectrum are sensitive to the ratio: the relative heights of the first three peaks pin down the dark-to-ordinary ratio with high precision. Planck's measurement is the value the previous chapter staked the framework on: 5.38 ± 0.15.

The fourth line is *large-scale structure*. The cosmic web — galaxies clustered into filaments and sheets, with vast voids between — formed under the combined gravitational influence of all the mass in the early universe, dark and ordinary. N-body simulations that include both kinds of mass reproduce the observed cosmic structure with high fidelity. Simulations with only ordinary matter do not reproduce it at all. The gravitational influence of the dark sector is required to produce the universe's structure as we see it.

These four lines converge. They give consistent values for the cosmic dark-to-ordinary ratio. They are independent measurements drawing on different physical processes at different cosmic epochs. They tell a coherent story.

What they do not tell us is *what dark matter is*. They tell us only that there is mass that gravitates without emitting light, and that there is roughly five times more of it than there is ordinary matter. The framework of this book is one possible structural reading of that empirical situation. The alternative readings — most of them invoking new particle species — have spent the past four decades looking for what they predict, and have not found it.

## §3 The Particle Hypothesis That Hasn't Worked

The dominant theoretical approach to dark matter, since the 1980s, has been to postulate a new particle. The candidate particle would be massive, electrically neutral, and weakly interacting — interacting at most through the weak nuclear force and gravity, never through electromagnetism. Such a particle would gravitate without emitting light, which is what dark matter does. It would also, if produced in the early universe in roughly the right abundance, leave behind the cosmic ratio we observe.

The most popular candidate, for decades, was the *weakly-interacting massive particle* — a WIMP. WIMPs at masses ranging from roughly 10 GeV up to a few TeV would have been produced thermally in the early universe and would have settled into something like the observed dark-matter abundance through standard particle-physics processes. The picture was well-motivated, mathematically clean, and led to an obvious experimental programme: build very sensitive detectors deep underground, shielded from cosmic rays, and wait for a WIMP to scatter off an atomic nucleus and deposit a small amount of energy. The energy deposit would be tiny but detectable.

This programme has been pursued with extraordinary effort. The XENON, LUX, PandaX, and DAMA experiments — among others — have each grown more sensitive by orders of magnitude over successive generations. The most recent generation can detect single WIMP-nucleus collisions with sensitivities far better than the original WIMP-mass-and-interaction-strength predictions required.

They have found nothing.

This is not a failure of one experiment. It is the consistent result across multiple experiments, multiple target materials, multiple mass ranges. The original WIMP parameter space — the range of WIMP masses and interaction strengths that the simplest theoretical models predicted — has been definitively ruled out. The remaining parameter space is parts of theory space that were never strongly motivated, kept alive by adjusting the candidate particle's mass or coupling away from where it was originally expected.

Other particle candidates — axions, sterile neutrinos, primordial black holes, asymmetric dark matter — have similar status. Each has a parameter space; some experimental programmes have ruled out parts of those spaces; the candidates have retreated into corners of theory space that are progressively less natural and less testable. The decades-long search has not produced a positive result for any candidate.

A natural reaction is to consider whether the search itself is misdirected. The framework of this book takes that step seriously. If dark matter is not a particle in our orientation, then no detector built in our orientation will find one. The negative results are not failures. They are correct results: there is nothing in our orientation to find.

## §4 The Other Trouser

What dark matter is, on the framework's reading, is the matter content of the other orientation, whose gravitational curvature registers in our orientation through the cross-thickness coupling.

Recall the bilateral picture from Chapter 7. The universe is one manifold with two orientations, related by CPN symmetry, sharing the Janus boundary at *N* = 0. We occupy one orientation. The other has its own matter — its own galaxies, its own large-scale structure — under the same physics, evolving in its own forward direction of time. Recall from Chapter 9 the cross-thickness coupling: the geometry of the manifold itself knits the two orientations together, and gravitational curvature on either orientation registers across the manifold's N-thickness in the other orientation's metric. This registration is mediated through the unresolved neutrino's bilateral wavefunction, the only species in the framework whose quantum state has support across both orientations.

What we observe gravitationally in our orientation has two contributions. The first is the curvature sourced by the ordinary matter on our side — the stars, the gas, the planets, the things our telescopes can directly observe. The second is the curvature sourced by the matter on the *other* side, registered into our orientation's metric through the cross-thickness coupling. The total gravitational signal is the sum of the two contributions. The dark-to-ordinary ratio is the ratio of these two contributions.

The previous chapter computed that ratio: κ = κ₀ / (1 − κ₀) ≈ 5.50. The structural argument is that κ₀, the bare coupling, is set geometrically by the Hopf fibration on the shared S³ boundary; that the closure step's geometric series resums the self-referential feedback of the coupling acting on its own contribution; and that the result is the cosmic dark-to-ordinary ratio Planck measures.

The chapter's title, "The Other Trouser," is the framework's gentle way of pointing at this picture. Dark matter is not particles. It is not a new species hiding in the dark of our universe. It is the gravitational presence of the matter on the other side of the manifold's thickness — the side whose existence is structurally forced by the framework's geometry, whose contents we cannot reach, whose light we cannot see, but whose mass registers in our gravitational equations through the cross-thickness coupling. The other trouser's matter is not visible to us, but it is present, and it is part of why our universe looks the way it does.

This is a structural claim, not a metaphor. The framework's gravitational equations have specific form. The cross-thickness coupling has specific strength κ₀. The closure step has specific algebraic content. The prediction κ = 5.50 is a consequence of the structural commitments, not an interpretation overlaid on them. The framework either reads the universe correctly or doesn't, and that depends on whether the universe behaves as the framework's bilateral picture says it should.

## §5 The Coherence Length

The framework's reading of dark matter has a specific testable prediction beyond κ = 5.50. The cross-thickness coupling, mediated through the unresolved neutrino's bilateral wavefunction, has a *coherence length* set by the neutrino's free-streaming distance — the typical scale over which an unresolved neutrino's wavefunction maintains its bilateral structure before decoherence and gravitational scattering disrupt the coherent superposition.

This scale is roughly forty megaparsecs — about a hundred and thirty million light-years.

To put that in context: a typical dwarf galaxy is a few thousand light-years across. A typical large galaxy like our own Milky Way is about a hundred thousand light-years across. A typical galaxy cluster is a few million light-years across. A typical large-scale structure (a filament or supervoid in the cosmic web) is tens to hundreds of millions of light-years across. The framework's coherence length sits roughly at the boundary between galaxy-cluster scale and large-scale-structure scale.

What this means physically: below the coherence length, the cross-thickness coupling fluctuates significantly. The bilateral wavefunction is intact at small scales, but the coupling's contribution to the gravitational signal varies from place to place, depending on local conditions of the unresolved neutrino's distribution. Above the coherence length, the coupling averages out. The cosmic-scale signal is the geometric mean of the framework's structural calculation; below the coherence length, the local signal can deviate substantially from that mean.

The prediction this gives the framework is sharp. *Below* the coherence length — at galaxy and small-cluster scales — the dark-to-ordinary ratio should show wide scatter from system to system. Some systems should be dominated by dark matter; others should have very little. *Above* the coherence length — at large-cluster and cosmic scales — the ratio should converge tightly to 5.50, with scatter only as wide as the measurement uncertainty.

This is a specific, testable structural claim, and the next section walks the comparison with observation.

## §6 Galaxies, Clusters, and the Scatter

The observational picture of dark-matter scatter, across cosmic scales, lines up with the framework's coherence-length prediction in striking detail.

At dwarf galaxy scales — systems much smaller than the coherence length — the dark-matter fractions show wild variation. Some dwarf galaxies are almost entirely dark matter; their visible stars are a tiny perturbation on the gravitational signal of an enormous dark halo. Others show very little dark matter; their stellar dynamics can be reproduced with not much extra mass at all. The diversity is one of the most striking and least explained observational facts about dwarf galaxies. Why should one ultra-faint dwarf have a hundred times the dynamical mass of its visible content while another, similar in luminosity and apparent size, has only twice? The standard particle-dark-matter picture has no clean explanation. The diversity is sometimes attributed to environmental effects — tidal stripping, accretion history, baryonic feedback — but the explanations are post-hoc, and none of them naturally produce the observed range.

The framework's reading is that this scatter is structural. Below the coherence length, the cross-thickness coupling fluctuates pointwise, depending on local conditions of the unresolved neutrino's bilateral wavefunction. Some dwarf galaxies sit at points where the local cross-thickness contribution is large, and they appear dark-matter-dominated. Others sit at points where the contribution is small, and they appear nearly free of dark matter. The scatter is the framework's prediction, not a deviation from it.

At galaxy-cluster scales — systems comparable to or larger than the coherence length — the dark-matter fractions converge. The diversity that haunts dwarf galaxies essentially disappears. Galaxy clusters have dark-to-ordinary mass ratios that cluster tightly around the cosmic value, with deviations smaller than the natural-system-to-system variation in baryonic content. This is exactly what the coherence-length argument predicts: at scales above the coherence length, the cross-thickness coupling averages out, and the ratio converges to the framework's structural prediction.

The standard treatment of dwarf-galaxy scatter is that the systems are too small for cosmological N-body simulations to predict reliably, that their dark matter halos are easily disrupted, and that the wide range of observed dark-matter fractions is a post-hoc consequence of complicated environmental physics. This is a real area of active research. The framework's reading offers a different account: the scatter is a structural prediction of the coherence-length argument, and the observational pattern — wide scatter below the coherence scale, tight convergence above — is what the framework predicts, not a complication for it.

The pattern, as it emerges from upcoming galaxy surveys (the Vera C. Rubin Observatory's Legacy Survey, the Euclid space telescope, future spectroscopic surveys), will sharpen the test. The framework predicts that as these surveys map the dark-matter content of billions of systems across the full range of scales, the distribution of dark-matter fractions will show the specific transition: variable below ~40 megaparsecs, convergent above. If the observational distribution matches this transition cleanly, the coherence-length prediction is confirmed. If the observational distribution shows wide scatter at all scales, or no scatter at any scale, or a transition at a substantially different scale, the framework's specific claim about the coherence length is wrong, and the framework needs to explain why.

This is the kind of thing the framework is supposed to be doing: making specific structural predictions whose observational signature is unambiguous, and putting itself in a position where future data either confirms or breaks the prediction.

## §7 Lensing and the Bullet Cluster

One of the most striking pieces of evidence for dark matter is the *Bullet Cluster*. In 2006, observations of two galaxy clusters that had recently collided showed a clean separation between the gravitational mass distribution (mapped through gravitational lensing) and the ordinary baryonic mass distribution (mapped through X-ray emission from the hot gas in the clusters). The hot gas, which is the bulk of the ordinary matter in galaxy clusters, had collided and been slowed by electromagnetic interactions; the dark mass, which appeared not to interact electromagnetically with the hot gas, had passed through the collision and continued on its original trajectory.

This observation is often called definitive evidence for the existence of dark matter. The gravitational mass is somewhere; the electromagnetically-interacting matter is somewhere else; the two distributions have separated, exactly as they would if the gravitational mass were not subject to the same electromagnetic interactions that slowed the hot gas.

The framework's reading of the Bullet Cluster is consistent with this picture, but with a different ontological claim. The gravitational mass that passed through the collision unimpeded is the curvature contribution from the matter on the other orientation, registered into our orientation through the cross-thickness coupling. The matter on the other orientation is unaffected by electromagnetic interactions with our orientation's hot gas — there is no electromagnetic coupling between orientations at all, since photons live in their own orientation. So the cross-thickness gravitational signal, in our orientation's measurements, behaves exactly as if it were a non-interacting dark mass: it tracks the gravitational potential of the other orientation's matter distribution, it does not interact with our hot gas, it lenses our background light according to its registered mass.

The Bullet Cluster is, in the framework's reading, a direct demonstration that dark matter does not interact with our orientation's electromagnetism. This is what the framework predicts: dark matter, being curvature from the other orientation, has no electromagnetic coupling in our orientation. The observed separation is consistent with this. It is also consistent with the standard particle-dark-matter picture; the framework does not claim that the Bullet Cluster uniquely confirms its reading. What the framework claims is that the Bullet Cluster does not contradict its reading, and that the absence of any electromagnetic interaction with our orientation's matter is structurally forced rather than postulated.

Subsequent observations of cluster collisions have produced similar pictures. The MACS J0025.4 cluster, the El Gordo cluster, and several others have each shown the same structural fact: gravitational mass and baryonic mass separate during collisions, with the gravitational mass behaving exactly as it would if it were structurally decoupled from our orientation's electromagnetism. The framework's reading is consistent with all of these.

## §8 What's Coming

The chapter has established the framework's reading of dark matter as a structural claim about the bilateral manifold, with specific testable predictions beyond the cosmic ratio κ = 5.50.

The framework predicts that direct-detection experiments will continue to find nothing. The negative results from XENON, LUX, PandaX, and successor experiments are not failures; they are correct results. There is no dark-matter particle in our orientation to detect. Future generations of underground experiments will refine the limits and find nothing within them. The framework will face a real problem only if a positive detection is unambiguously confirmed; the framework's claim is that this will not happen.

The framework predicts that dwarf-galaxy dark-matter fractions will continue to show wide scatter, while galaxy-cluster fractions will converge tightly to the cosmic mean. The transition should occur at roughly the coherence length, ~40 megaparsecs. As galaxy surveys map increasingly large samples, the distribution should settle into the predicted pattern. A failure of the transition to appear, or a transition at a substantially different scale, would break the framework's specific claim.

The framework predicts that gravitational lensing will continue to show the dark sector behaving exactly as if it were structurally decoupled from our orientation's electromagnetism. Cluster-collision observations should continue to show the gravitational mass and baryonic mass separating cleanly during collisions. No such observation has yet contradicted the framework; the framework's claim is that none will.

The framework predicts that the cosmic dark-to-ordinary ratio, as measured by CMB-S4 in the 2030s with twice Planck's precision, will converge to 5.50. Whether the small residual at the κ₀ level (the 0.36% gap that the framework's current corpus does not explain through any positive mechanism) is real or measurement noise will be answered then. If CMB-S4 confirms 5.50 within its tighter uncertainty, the framework's load-bearing prediction is confirmed at higher significance. If it converges to a different value at >3σ, the framework faces a real residual and the corpus's open structural research will need to identify what was missed.

Chapter 11 (*The Octavo*) will summarise the framework's predictions in a single catalogue. κ joins the cosmic mean Hubble parameter H₀ = 67.6 km/s/Mpc, the spectral index n_s = 0.9685, the tensor-to-scalar ratio r = 0.003, the atmospheric mixing angle θ₂₃ = 45°, exactly three generations of fermions, the existence and matter-favouring sign of the baryon asymmetry, and the dark-energy equation of state w = −1 (with the apparent low-redshift departure explained as void-enhancement, as the popular paper §12 developed). Each is a separate structural prediction. Each is testable. The framework's overall standing depends on the combined empirical record, not on any single match.

The chapter's closing thought. The framework is, in a specific sense, a deeply conservative theory. It does not introduce new particles, new forces, new spacetime dimensions, or new fundamental constants beyond those the Standard Model and General Relativity already inherit. It introduces one new geometric scalar — the boundary expansion field |Φ_NTE| — and one structural commitment — that the universe is a closed manifold with a bilateral N-thickness, related by CPN symmetry. From those minimal additions, the framework reads enough of the universe's structure to make specific quantitative predictions for cosmological observables that the standard treatments do not derive. Dark matter, in the framework's reading, is not a new species we have not yet found. It is the gravitational signature of geometry the framework has had all along.

The other trouser, in Pratchett's metaphor, is the leg of the universe we did not walk down. In the framework's structural account, it is the orientation we do not occupy — sharing the seam at the Janus boundary, evolving under the same physics, hosting its own galaxies and stars and structure, never reachable by any signal we could send. We feel its mass through the cross-thickness coupling. We do not see its light. The framework's claim is that this is enough — that dark matter is, structurally, exactly this and nothing more, and that the cosmic patterns of mass we measure are what the bilateral manifold's geometry must produce.

Whether the framework reads the universe correctly is, ultimately, what the next decade of observations will settle.
