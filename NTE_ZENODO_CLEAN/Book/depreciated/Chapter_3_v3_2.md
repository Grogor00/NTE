# Chapter 3 — A Closed Universe

## *The Destination*

> *He stares fixedly at the Destination.*
> — Terry Pratchett, *The Colour of Magic*

---

The previous two chapters arrived at a structural claim: the photon's null-time bridge and the entanglement bridge are aspects of one geometric object, and the geometry that produces both is the geometry of an S³. This chapter takes that claim and asks the obvious next question — what does it mean to live on an S³? What kind of universe is that, and how is it different from the universe most of us were taught to picture?

The honest answer is that S³ is one of the harder geometric objects to picture, because our visual system evolved to work with the kind of three-dimensional space we walk through, and S³ is something else. The good news is that we don't need to picture it directly. We can build up our understanding by analogy with simpler cases, and by the end of the chapter the picture will be clear enough to support the rest of the book. The bad news is that one of the most natural mental pictures — the one that usually shows up in popular science writing about higher dimensions — is wrong, and we'll need to dismantle it before going further.

## The Picture That Doesn't Work

When physics writers explain the idea that our universe might be the surface of a higher-dimensional sphere, they often reach for the balloon analogy. Imagine an inflating balloon, the writer says. We live on the surface of the balloon. As the balloon inflates, the surface stretches, and points painted on it move apart. That's why distant galaxies are receding from us. The balloon is the universe.

This is a useful picture for some purposes. It captures expansion as a property of the surface itself rather than as motion through a pre-existing space. It explains why every observer sees themselves at the centre of the recession — every point on a balloon is equally central to the inflation. But the balloon picture has a hidden assumption that becomes a problem when we take it seriously: the balloon has an *inside*. The inside contains air. The balloon is inflating by being filled.

The framework this book develops makes a different claim. The universe we live in is not the surface of something. It is not the boundary of an interior region we can't see. There is no inside. The S³ is everything — the entire physical universe — and the question of what's "inside" the S³ does not have an answer in the conventional sense, because the conventional sense presupposes a kind of structure the framework explicitly denies.

This is the bit that takes work to internalise. Almost everyone's first picture of "the universe is an S³" includes some version of "and outside the S³ is..." or "and inside the S³ is...". Both pictures are wrong. The correct picture is: the S³ is closed, finite in volume but boundless in extent, and there is no exterior or interior territory we could in principle access if we had better instruments. The S³ is not a thing in a larger space. It is the space.

To get there, we need a better analogy.

## The Right Analogy: An Ant on a Ball

Imagine an ant living on the surface of a ball. The ant is small, the ball is large from the ant's perspective, and the ant has no awareness of any direction except along the surface. It can walk forward, backward, left, right — any direction within the surface — but it has no sense of "up" or "down" out of the surface. For the ant, those words do not name directions. They are not unavailable; they are not concepts.

If the ant walks in what seems to it like a straight line, eventually it comes back to where it started. The surface is closed — finite in total area, but with no edge or boundary. The ant cannot walk off the ball, not because something stops it but because there is no off in any direction the ant can move. From within the ant's experience, the surface extends in every direction without limit.

Now the crucial part. We, looking at the ant from our three-dimensional vantage, can see that the ball has an interior. The ball is full of something — air, plastic, whatever. There is also a region outside the ball — the room the ball is in. But neither the interior nor the exterior matters to the ant. The ant's universe, completely, is the surface. Everything the ant can in principle reach, observe, or interact with lives on that surface.

If we asked the ant — somehow — *"what is inside the ball?"* — the ant would have no good answer. Not because the ant is ignorant. Because *inside the ball* is not a place the ant can describe in terms of its own world. The ant's geometry is two-dimensional. The interior of the ball is a three-dimensional structure that does not exist in the ant's geometry.

This is the picture we need. We are the ant. Our universe is the surface. There is no interior, not because we cannot see it but because the question presupposes a kind of structure that does not apply to our actual situation. The mathematical description of our universe as the boundary of a four-dimensional ball is a mathematical fact about the topology — but the four-dimensional ball is not a physical place. There is no inside to point to. There is only the surface, and the surface is everything.

## Building Up by Analogy

With the ant in hand, let us walk through the dimensional ladder.

A *circle* — what mathematicians call S¹ — is a closed one-dimensional space. It has length but no width. It is finite (you can measure its total circumference) but boundless (a creature confined to it could walk forever in either direction without hitting an end).

A *sphere* — S² — is a closed two-dimensional space. Our ant lives on one. It has area but no thickness. It is finite (you can measure its total surface area) but boundless (the ant can walk forever).

A *three-sphere* — S³ — is a closed three-dimensional space. It has volume but no... well, here the analogy runs out of words, because we don't have a fourth-dimensional concept to add. S³ has volume, but it is closed in the same sense the circle and the sphere are closed: finite total volume, but no edge or boundary. A creature inside it — and we are such creatures — would experience the space locally as ordinary three-dimensional space, but if it travelled far enough in any direction it would (in principle) come back to where it started.

This is the picture. The universe we live in is, locally, three-dimensional space. It looks Euclidean for any reasonable distance scale, the way the surface of the earth looks flat for any walking distance. But globally, the universe is closed. It does not extend to infinity. It curls around on itself in a way our intuition cannot directly visualise, but which is mathematically straightforward. There is no edge of the universe. There is also no interior of the universe, in the sense of a region accessible by going perpendicular to space — because perpendicular to space, in the framework's claim, simply isn't a direction.

## What Expansion Means

The universe is expanding. Cosmologists have known this since Hubble's observations in the 1920s, and successive measurements have refined the rate at which the expansion is happening but never overturned the fact. Distant galaxies are receding from us, and from each other, and the further away they are the faster they recede.

In the standard textbook treatment, this expansion is sometimes presented as if galaxies were moving outward into pre-existing empty space. The framework rejects this picture, and so does most modern cosmology if you read carefully. There is no pre-existing space the galaxies move into. The expansion is happening to space itself.

In the S³ picture, the expansion has a clean geometric meaning. The S³ has a scale — a typical distance between any two points, set by the geometry. As the universe expands, that scale grows. Distances between fixed points on the S³ increase, not because the points are moving but because the metric is changing. If you painted two dots on the surface of a balloon and inflated it, the dots stay at the same locations on the rubber, but the rubber itself is stretching, so the distance between the dots grows. The balloon analogy fails for the universe because of the interior assumption — but this *one specific aspect* of it, the stretching of the metric, is the right intuition.

The framework parameterises the expansion not by time but by a number called the e-fold count, written *N*. Each e-fold is one factor of *e* (about 2.7) by which the universe has grown. After two e-folds, the universe is e² ≈ 7.4 times larger than at the start; after sixty e-folds, it is e⁶⁰ ≈ 10²⁶ times larger. *N* is a pure number — a count of how much expansion has occurred — and it requires no clock to define. You don't need to know what time is in order to count expansions.

A reader who follows cosmology may want to flag a concern at this point. Current observation has the universe consistent with *flat* to within roughly a part per thousand. The Planck satellite's measurements give a curvature density Ω_K consistent with zero, with experimental error too small for the framework's S³ closure to hide in. So is the framework's commitment to a closed S³ in tension with what we observe?

The answer turns on a fact about spherical geometry. Local curvature on an S³ of radius *r* scales as 1/*r*². A small sphere is sharply curved everywhere on its surface. A large sphere is curved less steeply. In the limit, a sufficiently large S³ becomes locally indistinguishable from flat — the curvature, while still nonzero everywhere, is small enough that no local measurement of finite precision can detect it. The framework's commitment to closure is consistent with the observation that the universe is locally flat, *provided* the S³ curvature radius is large enough that current measurements bound it rather than refute it. We are the ant on a ball that is large enough that, at the ant's scale, the ground beneath its feet looks flat — even though, in principle, the ball is closed. The framework postulates closure; observation has so far been unable to rule it out, and future precision cosmology that directly bounds the curvature radius is one of the framework's testable predictions.

This matters because of the central claim from Chapter 1: time is not the primary parameter. *N* is. What we experience as time is a consequence of expansion, with the boundary expansion field |Φ_NTE| parameterising the rate at which proper time accumulates per e-fold. Chapter 4 will work this out in detail. For now, hold the picture: *N* is the geometric count of expansion, *t* is what emerges from *N* with |Φ_NTE| setting the rate, and the universe is an expanding closed three-dimensional space.

## What "The Other Side" Means

A reader who has been working with the ant analogy may notice a question. The ant lives on a ball. The ball has an antipodal point — the point on the opposite side of the ball from wherever the ant is. If the ant walks long enough in any direction, eventually it reaches the antipode. So if our universe is an S³, doesn't it have an antipodal point too? What's there?

The answer is yes, geometrically — and we should be careful about what this does and does not imply. Here are the two things worth saying.

The first is geometric. The antipodal point on an S³ is not a different region or a different universe. It is just the point geometrically furthest from wherever you are, on the same closed surface. If the universe is an S³, there is some point — billions of light-years away, well beyond any distance we could ever signal across — which is the antipodal point of the location you are sitting in. That point is part of the same universe, made of the same fabric, governed by the same laws. It is, however, almost certainly empty of any structure that matters for our local physics, in the same way that a point at random in any other large region of the universe is empty: the universe is mostly empty space.

The second concerns a misreading worth heading off, because the framework's later development uses related but distinct geometric structure. The framework will make a strong claim, in Chapter 10, about what looks to us like *dark matter* — the invisible mass whose gravity shapes galaxies. The claim will be that dark matter is best understood as gravitational coupling to a *bilateral* structure of the manifold, mediated by neutrinos. A reader who has been thinking about antipodal points might naturally hear "bilateral structure" and infer that the dark-matter mechanism is about gravitational influence from matter on the antipodal side of the S³ — that is, matter located at the geometrically furthest spatial point from us. This is *not* what the framework claims. The bilateral structure that does the work in the framework is something different: a thickness or orientation feature of the manifold itself, not a separation between two spatial regions. The closest everyday analogy is the two sides of a sheet of paper — adjacent at every point, distinguishable by which face you are on rather than by being far apart spatially. The two sides of the manifold are like that: adjacent at every spatial location, but oriented differently in a way the framework will explain. Chapter 10 will develop this carefully and the term that will be doing the work is *CPN-conjugate orientation*, which is not the same kind of thing as a spatial antipode.

What this means for now: the antipodal-spatial geometry of S³ is real and interesting and does not, by itself, do the dark-matter work. The geometry that does the dark-matter work is a different feature of the same closed three-dimensional manifold, and Chapter 10 is where it lands. The point of mentioning the antipodal point in this chapter is just to flag that closed manifolds have this structure, that the framework is happy to acknowledge it, and that closure does not mean "trapped" — the universe is one closed three-dimensional space, finite in volume but with no edge or boundary, and every point on it is part of the same manifold.

Both sides of the S³, all the matter, all the radiation, all the geometry — lives on one closed three-dimensional space. There is no inside, no outside. There is the S³, and the S³ is everything.

## Where This Leaves the Photon and the Entangled Pair

The two phenomena from Chapters 1 and 2 sharpen under this picture.

The photon's journey from a distant star to your eye does not happen in a static space across which the photon moves. It happens on the surface of an expanding S³, with the photon riding the wavefront of that expansion. The photon has zero engagement with the boundary expansion field — no proper time accumulates for it as the universe expands — and so from its frame, the moment of emission and the moment of absorption are the same event. The forty million light-years of distance you measure is the geometric extent of how much the S³ has expanded while the photon has been on its wavefront. The photon hasn't *waited*; it has simply been on the wavefront the whole time, and the wavefront is what's been moving.

The entangled pair is, in the same picture, a single object on the same S³. The structure linking the two laboratory measurements is one geometric feature of the closed three-dimensional space. The two particles are not separated by territory the structure has to span; they are endpoints of one feature, and the feature does not require anything to travel between them because it does not have a between in the conventional sense. From the perspective of the structure itself — which is a zero-proper-time feature, by virtue of involving null-time correlations — the two endpoints are aspects of one situation, not two situations that need to coordinate.

Both observations come down to the same thing: the universe is a closed three-dimensional space, and certain geometric features of that space — features at zero proper time — have the property that they connect distant points without travelling through the territory between them. Photons and entangled pairs are the two cases physicists have studied most carefully, but they are aspects of the same phenomenon.

## Towards Time as Expansion

The picture is now in place. Closed three-dimensional space. No interior, no exterior. Expansion as a change in the metric, parameterised by *N*. Photons riding the wavefront. Entanglement as zero-proper-time features of the same surface. Time, somehow, emerging from expansion through the boundary expansion field — but this last piece is the one we have not yet worked out.

The next chapter takes that piece up directly. What does it mean for time to emerge from expansion? What role does the boundary expansion field play in setting the rate? And what does this tell us about the difference between the world we observe and the world the framework actually describes?

The Pratchett line at the head of this chapter is from *The Colour of Magic*. The world Pratchett describes is on a turtle, and the turtle is going somewhere — somewhere the inhabitants cannot reach by any means available to them, somewhere they call only the Destination. What matters in that picture is not what the Destination is. What matters is that the inhabitants of the world have only the world to work with, and the geometry of their situation is the geometry of being on a surface that is moving. They do not have access to the road. They have only the surface, and the question of where it is going.

The framework of this book takes a similar posture, scaled up by one dimension. We do not have access to whatever the universe is "going through" in the sense of the deeper geometry. We have the universe — the S³ surface, expanding, carrying us along with it — and the question of what we can learn about its structure from inside it. The next chapter takes a closer look at the moving part of that picture.
