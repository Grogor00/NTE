The framework, on its own account, can take this structure and derive a specific number from it — the ratio of cross-thickness gravitational contribution to local-baryon contribution. Not approximately. Not within an order of magnitude. A specific number, fixed by the framework's geometry, with no free parameters and no dimensional inputs. That number is 5.50. The number Planck measures for the dark-matter-to-baryon ratio in our universe is 5.38, with an uncertainty of about 0.15.

This chapter writes the derivation down.

The chapter is staked on this number in a way the rest of the book is not. The other chapters develop structural readings of phenomena that are already known — quantum mechanics's Born rule, the gauge structure of the Standard Model, the cascade that gives particles their masses. The match, as it happens, holds. The chapter walks the reader through why.

## §1 — The Empirical Mystery

In our universe, dark matter outweighs ordinary matter by roughly 5.4 to 1. The number is one of the most precisely measured in cosmology. The Planck satellite, in its 2018 data release, pinned it down to 5.38 ± 0.15 from the temperature fluctuations of the cosmic microwave background and their angular distribution across the sky. Independent gravitational evidence — galaxy rotation curves, gravitational lensing, the growth of large-scale structure — corroborates the cosmological measurement within its own less-precise bands. There is something out there, gravitating in the way mass does, that is not the ordinary matter the Standard Model describes, and it has roughly five and a half times the mass.

The Standard Model has nothing to say about this number.

It is worth pausing on what that means. Particle physics has produced, over a century of work, the most precise quantitative theory of fundamental physics ever assembled. It knows what baryons are. It knows how they interact, what they decay into, what holds them together. But for the cosmic ratio of dark matter to ordinary matter — a number that sits at the centre of structure formation, galaxy dynamics, and cosmic evolution — the Standard Model offers nothing. There is no candidate particle in the Standard Model that could be the dark matter. There is no derivation, from any combination of Standard Model parameters, of why the ratio should be 5.4. The number is an inference from gravitational observation, fitted into the cosmological model as an input rather than emerging from the theory as an output.

This has not stopped people from proposing extensions of the Standard Model that would fit the ratio. Various candidates exist: weakly-interacting massive particles at specific masses, axions at specific decay constants, primordial black holes at specific abundances, sterile neutrinos at specific mixing angles. Each candidate, if it exists, gives a way to *fit* the observed ratio by tuning the candidate's parameters. None of them *predicts* the number from inputs that were not chosen to make it come out right. The dark-matter-to-baryon ratio, in every candidate framework, is a parameter to be matched, not a number to be derived.

The framework of this book is going to do something different. It will predict the ratio from inputs that come from elsewhere — the bilateral structure introduced in Chapter 7, the unresolved neutrino developed in Chapter 8, the Hopf fibration on S³ from Chapter 6. None of these were chosen to make the ratio come out right; they were developed for other structural reasons in earlier chapters. This chapter shows that when the framework's account of cross-thickness gravity is run through to a number, the number that falls out is 5.50.

A note up front about what the framework's structure does and does not constrain. The bilateral structure makes the ratio non-negative — a closed series with a positive coupling has no negative fixed point — and the closure step we will reach in §5 only converges if the bare coupling is less than one, so the ratio is also finite. A naive expectation under bilateral structure alone, with both sectors carrying the same baryonic content, would predict equal contribution from each side and a ratio of one. A no-bilateral-structure picture would predict zero. The framework's specific structure, with the self-referential closure §3 will explain and §5 will resum, picks out a particular value: 5.50. The match with Planck's 5.38 is a test the framework could have failed — not because the ratio could have been any number imaginable, but because *given the framework's structural commitments*, 5.50 is forced, and the question is whether nature agrees.

The chapter is staked on a specific number whose value the framework's structure determines. The reader should feel this from here forward. The remaining sections develop the structural argument that produces 5.50, and the final sections take the match with observation seriously without overclaiming what a single match can mean.

## §2 — The Bilateral Picture

Chapter 7 introduced the framework's bilateral structure. At the gauge birth — the moment the Standard Model gauge symmetries switched on and proper time crystallised — the universe locked into one of two CPN-conjugate orientations of the manifold's N-thickness. Both orientations exist. They are not two separate manifolds glued together at an edge, and they are not two regions of one space connected by some sort of bridge. They are two faces of one geometric structure: a single closed manifold with thickness in the e-fold direction N, with our orientation occupying one face and the conjugate orientation occupying the other, joined at the Janus boundary at N=0 that neither occupies but both share.

What the lay reader needs to carry forward from Chapter 7: the manifold has thickness; the two orientations are the two faces of that thickness; the Janus boundary at N=0 is the shared edge. The two orientations do not share photons — electromagnetic interactions stay in their own orientation — but they share the geometric substrate the manifold sits on, and they share the Janus boundary at the heart of the bilateral structure.

This is the framework's account of what dark matter is. What we observe gravitationally as "dark" — the matter responsible for galaxy rotation curves, gravitational lensing, structure formation — is, on the framework's reading, the matter content of the other orientation, whose curvature registers across the manifold's N-thickness in our orientation's gravitational equations. There is no mechanism conveying matter "into" our universe. What crosses the thickness is curvature, not matter. Gravity is geometric, not mediated by particles; the cross-thickness effect works because both orientations share the same underlying manifold geometry, and curvature on one face is part of the geometry the other face inhabits.

A naive expectation under this picture: if both orientations have the same matter content, which the framework's CPN symmetry forces, then the dark-matter-to-baryon ratio should equal one. Equal mirror, equal contribution, factor of one. But the ratio is not one — it is 5.4. Something is amplifying the cross-thickness contribution by a factor of roughly five. The rest of the chapter is about what that amplification is and why it has the specific structural form it does.

A pedagogical word, because the picture courts a misreading the framework needs to head off. The literature has a thing called "mirror matter" — a hypothesised second sector of particles that mirrors the visible sector, with its own forces and its own dynamics. The framework's bilateral picture is *not* mirror matter in that sense. Mirror matter is a separate field theory with its own assumptions and its own parameters; the framework's bilateral structure is a geometric fact about the manifold the universe sits on, with no separate parameters and no separate dynamics. The two orientations are not two universes that happen to share gravity. They are the two faces of one universe, and what the framework derives in this chapter would be impossible in a mirror-matter picture because the structural amplification has its origin in geometry, not in any separate sector's dynamics.

## §3 — The Geometric Coupling and the Bilaterally-Supported Structure

The two orientations of the universe are not separate places. They are two faces of one geometric structure — the manifold's N-thickness, with the Janus boundary at N=0 belonging to neither orientation but shared by both. Gravity reaches between the orientations not because anything travels across, but because the geometry connects them. The connection is *topological*: a structural fact about how the framework's primary three-sphere knits the two orientations of the thickness together. The framework calls this the cross-thickness coupling, and its strength is set by the geometric structure on the shared boundary.

The cross-thickness coupling is geometric and is always present. But it only registers in the framework's gravitational equations where there is a *structure that lives across both orientations* to register against. Most particles do not. The fully resolved fermions — electrons, quarks, every species that completed the Hopf-projection cascade Chapter 7 walked through — have wavefunctions confined to one of the two orientations. Their stress-energy belongs to one face only. The cross-thickness coupling exists in the background geometry, but for these species there is no structural handle on which it can act; the resolved fermions live on one face, and their stress-energy contributes to one face's gravitational field, and the cross-thickness coupling has no role in their gravitational accounting.

The unresolved neutrino is the exception. Chapter 8 developed in detail what its non-resolution amounts to: the Hopf-projection completion that fixes other species at a definite identity failed to finish for the neutrino, leaving its wavefunction in a configuration that does not commit to either particle or antiparticle, and — crucially for this chapter — leaving its wavefunction with *support on both orientations* of the manifold's N-thickness simultaneously. The neutrino has bilateral support not because its Yukawa coupling is small, although it is, but because its Majorana character with the particle-antiparticle binary unresolved leaves the topological structure that orientation-locking requires unavailable to the species. The neutrino's wavefunction lives across the thickness; its stress-energy sources curvature on both orientations at once.

The geometric coupling and the bilaterally-supported structure are distinct things. The coupling is a topological fact about the manifold's N-thickness — a feature of the geometry that exists whether or not anything bilaterally-supported is present to act on. The bilateral structure is the unresolved neutrino's wavefunction having support across the thickness — a feature of one specific species. The framework's gravitational equations couple these together: the topological coupling becomes manifest where there is a bilaterally-supported structure for it to act on, and the unresolved neutrino is the species in our universe that provides that structure. *The neutrino does not carry the cross-thickness coupling; it does not mediate the coupling; it is the structure that makes the coupling show up in the equations describing what we measure.*

Once this is said carefully, the chapter's hardest pedagogical move becomes available. The cross-thickness coupling acts on whatever bilaterally-supported structure exists. The unresolved neutrino, however, has no gauge quantum numbers that would let it discriminate between curvature sourced by baryons in our orientation and curvature sourced by baryons in the other. The neutrino has no electric charge to feel one sector's electromagnetism rather than the other's; no colour charge; no weak isospin in its locked sense. From its position straddling the thickness, what it engages with is simply curvature. So whatever stress-energy is present on either orientation gets registered in the cross-thickness coupling, *including* the curvature that the coupling has just contributed from the other side back to our own.

This is the self-referential feature §5 will resum. It is structurally forced, not postulated. Given a geometric coupling that acts on a structure with no gauge quantum numbers to discriminate, the coupling reaches everything that gravitates — including its own contribution coming back. The feedback loop is built into the geometry; the closure step §5 develops is how the framework adds the loop up.

The cross-thickness coupling has a specific strength, which the framework calls κ₀, set by the geometric structure on the shared boundary. That strength is what the next section derives.

## §4 — The Bare Coupling

This is the chapter's hardest section to land at lay register, and the chapter will move carefully through it. The end of the section will give the reader a specific number — κ₀ ≈ 0.846 — produced from geometry alone, with no free parameters. The number is set by three structural facts about the framework's primary geometry on S³ and the unresolved neutrino's relation to it. Each fact has been developed in an earlier chapter; this section pulls them together.

Recall from Chapter 6 that the framework's geometry on S³ — the closed three-sphere that organises both quantum-mechanical state space and the structure of the manifold's expansion — comes with a particular geometric apparatus called the *Hopf fibration*. The Hopf fibration is the structural fact that S³ can be thought of as a collection of circles (each circle is a *fibre*) parameterised by a two-sphere S² (the *base*). The fibres are the unobservable U(1) phases the Born rule quotients out; the base S² is what physical states actually distinguish. The same Hopf structure that organises quantum mechanics organises, in this chapter, the cross-thickness coupling on the shared boundary. This is not a coincidence; it is what the framework expects of a structural primitive. One geometric object plays multiple roles.

The first fact the chapter needs. The framework asks a question of its geometry: across the shared S³ boundary, what is the angular distribution of the unresolved neutrino's quantum-mechanical density? The framework's structural commitments do not pick out any preferred direction. The neutrino is unresolved precisely because its Hopf projection did not complete, leaving its wavefunction smeared symmetrically across the fibre structure rather than pinned at any particular angular position. So the density must be *angularly uniform*. The geometric average of an angularly uniform density on the unit two-sphere has a specific value: it is the area-normalisation of the sphere, which is 1/√(4π). This factor is geometry, not parameter. It comes from the structural fact that a uniform density on the unit two-sphere, in the standard mathematical normalisation, projects to the lowest harmonic Y₀₀ = 1/√(4π). The framework's broader corpus calls this projection step the *Hopf monopole projection* — the geometric projection of the Hopf-fibred S³ down to its lowest harmonic on the base S². For the lay-register chapter, what matters is that the projection produces a specific number — 1/√(4π) — and that the number has no parameters in it.

The second fact. Why is there only one factor of 1/√(4π), or three, or some other count? Because the unresolved neutrino has *three* mass eigenmodes. This was developed in Chapter 8 as the source of flavour oscillation: the unresolved species decomposes into three eigenmodes corresponding to the three independent generators of the algebra called su(2), the algebra that organises the manifold's S³ structure and which the reader has met as the algebra underlying spin in quantum mechanics. The factor of three in the bare coupling comes from this dimensional count: three eigenmodes, each contributing the same monopole projection independently. For the κ₀ derivation specifically, only the count dim(su(2)) = 3 is load-bearing; the further structural question of how the three eigenmodes correspond to the three observed neutrino flavours is a separate corpus claim the framework reads as kin to this one but structurally distinct.

The third fact, and the framework's most distinctive technical move here. In a generic field-theoretic calculation, one might worry that higher harmonics on the two-sphere — corrections beyond the lowest harmonic Y₀₀ — also contribute to κ₀ at subleading order, and that the topological projection is only an approximation. The framework's response is that the lowest harmonic is *topologically protected*. The monopole projection of any normalised density on the sphere is the constant Y₀₀. This is a mathematical identity, not a calculational approximation: any density, however structured, projects to the same constant when its monopole component is taken. The technical corpus develops this through what is called the Gaunt coefficient calculation, which establishes mode-independence at the level of the integrals that would mix the lowest harmonic with higher modes — these integrals vanish for the relevant indices. The lay-register version: the projection step is *exact*. No correction can shift κ₀ from the topological value, because the topological value is what the projection structurally is.

Multiply. Three eigenmodes, each contributing the area-normalisation factor of the lowest harmonic on the sphere: κ₀ = 3 × 1/√(4π) = 3/√(4π) ≈ 0.846. This is the bare coupling, the strength of the cross-thickness coupling before the closure step's self-referential feedback is taken into account. It is set entirely by geometry: three eigenmodes from the unresolved neutrino's su(2) structure, the area-normalisation of the two-sphere from the Hopf monopole projection, the topological protection from the structural identity Y₀₀ satisfies. No tunable parameters.

At this point the framework has produced one number from pure geometry. The number is interesting but it is not yet the cosmological ratio. The closure step is what turns κ₀ into the observed value, and that is the next section's job.

## §5 — The Closure

Return to §3's self-referential feature. The cross-thickness coupling, acting on a bilaterally-supported structure with no gauge quantum numbers, attaches to *total* stress-energy on each orientation — including the contribution that arrives via the same coupling from the other orientation. This is what makes the closure step necessary, and the framework's broader corpus calls the step the *CPN-symmetric resummation* because the closure runs under the bilateral structure's CPN symmetry, where the two orientations contribute symmetrically and the result resums their mutual feedback.

In symbols, lightly. Let T denote the total stress-energy in our orientation. It has two contributions: the baryonic stress-energy T_b that lives in our orientation directly, and the cross-thickness contribution that arrives via the geometric coupling from the other orientation. The cross-thickness contribution is κ₀ times the total stress-energy in the other orientation, T'. The framework's CPN symmetry forces T' = T — both orientations have the same total stress-energy structure under the bilateral picture's mirror relation. So the total stress-energy in our orientation satisfies the algebra:

T = T_b + κ₀ T

Solve for T. Rearrange: T (1 − κ₀) = T_b. Divide: T = T_b / (1 − κ₀).

The dark-matter contribution to our orientation is the part of T that is not T_b — the cross-thickness piece, κ₀ T = κ₀ T_b / (1 − κ₀). The dark-matter-to-baryon ratio is therefore the cross-thickness piece divided by the baryonic piece, which works out to:

κ = κ₀ / (1 − κ₀)

Plug in. κ₀ = 3/√(4π) ≈ 0.846; (1 − κ₀) ≈ 0.154; κ ≈ 0.846 / 0.154 ≈ 5.50.

That is the prediction. The framework's geometry, run through the closure step, produces 5.50 with no free parameters.

A reader who has met geometric series in basic algebra will see something familiar in the algebra above. The closure step is, structurally, the resummation of a geometric series. Imagine running the cross-thickness coupling *perturbatively*, treating each round of feedback separately. At first order, the cross-thickness coupling contributes κ₀ T_b — a fraction κ₀ of the baryonic stress-energy from the other orientation crosses the thickness and registers on our side. At second order, the coupling sees its own first-order contribution from the other orientation and contributes another factor of κ₀ — total contribution κ₀² T_b. At third order, the coupling sees the second-order contribution coming back and contributes κ₀³ T_b. The total cross-thickness contribution, summed across all orders, is κ₀ T_b + κ₀² T_b + κ₀³ T_b + … — the geometric series whose closed form is κ₀ T_b / (1 − κ₀), which gives back the same answer the algebraic resummation produces.

This is the same geometric series the reader has met in basic algebra. What is structurally novel is that the geometric series here is not a calculational trick — it is *real* structural feedback. The cross-thickness coupling, by its nature as a geometric coupling acting on a structure with no gauge quantum numbers, *does* see its own contribution coming back from the other orientation. Each round of feedback is real. The closure step is the framework's way of summing all rounds at once, producing a finite answer because the bare coupling is less than one and the series therefore converges.

The amplification factor is worth dwelling on. From a bare coupling of 0.846 — less than unity, only just — the closure produces 5.50. The factor of amplification is roughly six. This is the amount by which the cross-thickness feedback enhances the bare bilateral contribution. The amplification is structural rather than fortuitous: it has the form of a geometric series, the bare coupling sits in the convergence regime by a comfortable margin, and the answer the series produces is precisely what we observe. The chapter should let the reader feel this. The number 5.50 is not the bare coupling itself; it is what the bare coupling becomes once the framework's geometry has been allowed to feed back on itself the way the structure forces it to.

What the framework has done in §§3–5 is one structural derivation. There are not two separate calculations converging on the same answer. There is one chain: §4's Hopf monopole projection produces κ₀ topologically; §5's CPN-symmetric resummation closes κ₀ → κ algebraically. Pathway B feeds Pathway A; the labels are useful in the technical corpus, but the chapter has no need to invoke them, because the structural fact is simpler than the labels make it sound. One bare coupling, set by geometry. One closure step, forced by the bilateral structure. One number falls out.

## §6 — Where the Number Comes From

It is worth taking stock before reaching for the comparison with observation.

The chapter's derivation chain has two structural steps. The first step takes the framework's geometry on the shared S³ boundary and produces the bare coupling κ₀ = 3/√(4π) by way of the Hopf monopole projection: angular uniformity gives the area-normalisation of the two-sphere; three eigenmodes from the unresolved neutrino's su(2) structure give the multiplicative count; topological protection of the lowest harmonic makes the projection exact rather than approximate. The second step takes the bare coupling and runs it through the CPN-symmetric self-consistency that the framework's bilateral structure forces, producing κ = κ₀/(1 − κ₀) ≈ 5.50 by geometric resummation. The two steps are sequential, not parallel — the closure step takes the bare coupling step's output as its input — and the chain as a whole is one structural derivation, not two independent confirmations.

What goes in:

1. The bilateral structure (CPN-conjugate orientations of one manifold's N-thickness, joined at the Janus boundary at N=0), from Chapter 7's gauge birth.
2. The unresolved neutrino as the bilaterally-supported structure, from Chapter 8's account of the incomplete Hopf-projection completion.
3. The Hopf fibration on S³ as the geometric primitive of the framework, from Chapter 6.
4. The principle that the cross-thickness coupling acts on total stress-energy, which follows in this chapter §3 from the unresolved neutrino having no gauge quantum numbers to discriminate among different sources of curvature.

What comes out:

One number. κ = κ₀ / (1 − κ₀) = (3/√(4π)) / (1 − 3/√(4π)) ≈ 5.50.

Nothing has been chosen to make this answer come out right. The bilateral structure was developed in Chapter 7 for reasons of gauge structure and the algebraic reduction at the gauge birth, not because the framework knew where the κ derivation would land. The unresolved neutrino was developed in Chapter 8 to explain the neutrino's tiny mass, its flavour oscillation, and its Majorana character, not to provide a bilaterally-supported structure for cross-thickness gravity. The Hopf fibration is in the framework because it is what the Born rule needs. Each input is doing structural work elsewhere in the corpus, and the κ derivation is what the inputs collectively produce when combined in the way the framework's geometry forces.

This is the framework's distinctive claim about this number, and the reason this chapter exists. The dark-matter-to-baryon ratio is not a parameter the framework matches; it is a number the framework's geometry calculates with zero free parameters and no dimensional inputs. The reader is asked to take this seriously not because the framework is right about everything else, but because if the framework is right *here*, this is the kind of agreement that is structurally informative.

## §7 — The 0.83σ Match

Side-by-side. The framework's prediction: κ = 5.50. Planck's measurement: κ = 5.38 ± 0.15. The difference is 0.12, which is about 0.83 standard deviations — well within statistical agreement between the framework's number and the universe's number.

What does this mean? A prediction at 0.83σ from observation is, in the standard scientific sense, a successful prediction. It is not a confirmation. A single agreement is consistent with the framework being correct; it is also consistent with the framework being wrong but coincidentally producing this number for an unrelated structural reason; it is also consistent with the universe being a place where the dark-matter-to-baryon ratio happens to sit at 5.38 and any number of frameworks could find their way to a number near it. The chapter must not claim the match "proves" the framework. The book's overall position — multiple predictions, multiple structural results, the framework standing or falling on the totality — is the right framing, and Chapter 11 will catalogue the broader prediction record.

What the match *is*, however, is real. The framework predicted a specific number from inputs not chosen to match it; the number agrees with the most precise cosmological measurement available, within the measurement's stated uncertainty. The reader is owed an honest assessment: this is the kind of agreement that, if it kept happening across enough independent predictions, would constitute evidence. One match is not a verdict. A sequence of matches at this calibre would be hard to dismiss.

A note on the residual. The κ-level difference between prediction and measurement is 0.12 — about 2.3 percent of the observed value. At the bare-coupling level, the residual is much smaller: matching κ_obs = 5.38 exactly would require shifting κ₀ from 0.846 to 0.843, a 0.36 percent adjustment. This is suggestively small, and earlier framework discussion identified candidate sources for it. Subsequent work has closed those candidates. The topological protection of the lowest harmonic makes corrections to κ₀ from finite-Yukawa-coupling effects exactly zero — a mathematical identity, not an approximation. The three-eigenmode count is topological. The closure step is algebraically exact. The framework, on its current corpus, does not identify a positive candidate explanation for the residual.

This is worth being clear about. The 0.83σ discrepancy is within Planck's measurement uncertainty, and the most honest framing is that the residual may be a genuine structural puzzle the framework has not yet identified, or it may be the size of measurement noise. The next-generation cosmic microwave background experiment, CMB-S4, will measure the dark-matter-to-baryon ratio with roughly twice Planck's precision. When CMB-S4's data are in, the residual will either resolve into agreement or sharpen into a discrepancy the framework has to account for. The framework's bet is the former. The chapter is not making a prediction beyond what it has already made; it is naming the open question and the test that will close it.

## §8 — What Is and Isn't Load-Bearing

The framework's discipline is to be explicit about what its predictions depend on and where they might fail. This section does that work for the κ derivation specifically.

Robust under the framework's reading:

- κ₀ = 3/√(4π) is structurally exact at the topological-projection step. The monopole projection of any normalised density is the constant Y₀₀, by mathematical identity. No physical correction mechanism can shift it.
- The three-eigenmode count is set by the dimension of the unresolved neutrino's su(2) mass-eigenmode structure. This is a topological count, not a phenomenological fit.
- The closure step's geometric series is algebraically exact given its inputs. The fixed-point solution T = T_b / (1 − κ₀) is forced by the structure of the equations, not chosen to land somewhere convenient.

The derivation as a whole rests on five structural inputs the framework's broader corpus names explicitly: the topological protection of the lowest harmonic Y₀₀ on the two-sphere; the three-eigenmode count from dim(su(2)) = 3; a Z₂-even delocalisation property of the unresolved neutrino's bilateral wavefunction; the validity of effective field theory at the relevant scales; and an action principle from which the framework's two-metric gravitational equations would follow rigorously. The first four of these are settled. The fifth is the framework's foundational vulnerability for this derivation.

This is worth being honest about. The framework has the equations the κ derivation runs on — the two-metric Einstein equations that describe how curvature on one orientation registers on the other through the cross-thickness coupling. These equations work in the working effective theory and produce the closure step's algebraic content correctly. What the framework does not yet have is the ground-up derivation of those equations from a single action principle — the foundational construction that would ensure Bianchi consistency, close the formalism, and place the κ derivation on the same kind of footing as derivations in established field theories. The broader corpus calls this Gap 4b. It is open structural research. The framework is honest about the gap; the κ derivation is what runs on the equations the framework has, and Gap 4b is the long-term programme to close the formalism's foundation.

Where the prediction *would fail*. The chapter has an obligation to identify what would have to be different to make the prediction not 5.50:

- If the bilateral symmetry were not CPN — say, only C without P or N — the equality T = T_b + κ₀ T' would not have T' = T forced by symmetry, and the closure step would not have its symmetric form.
- If the neutrino were *resolved* — that is, if the Hopf-projection completion succeeded for it as for the electron and the quarks — there would be no bilaterally-supported structure for the cross-thickness coupling to act on. The coupling would still exist as a geometric fact about the manifold, but it would have nothing to register in our orientation's gravitational equations.
- If the framework's primary geometric structure were not S³ — say, if the underlying geometry were toroidal rather than spherical — the angular-uniformity argument would not produce 1/√(4π) at the area-normalisation step, and the bare coupling would have a different value.
- If the unresolved neutrino had gauge quantum numbers that let it discriminate among sources of curvature, the cross-thickness coupling would not attach to total stress-energy in the way §3 develops, and the self-referential feedback that the closure step resums would not be structurally forced.

These are the framework's exposed flanks for this prediction. They are not hidden; they are named. The framework's bet is that none of these conditions is different from what the framework says, and that 5.50 is the number geometry produces. The reader is invited to take the bet seriously by tracking what the bet entails: agreement on bilateral structure, agreement on the neutrino's unresolved status, agreement on the framework's S³ geometry, agreement on the no-gauge-quantum-numbers structural fact about the unresolved neutrino's coupling.

What this means for the chapter is straightforward. The framework is making a claim that could be wrong, and the chapter has identified explicitly what would have to be different to make it wrong. The κ derivation is genuinely vulnerable in the way an empirical prediction is vulnerable — exposed to data, exposed to alternative readings of its inputs, exposed to refinement when better measurements arrive. The match it currently shows with Planck does not absolve it from this vulnerability; it places the derivation in the category of structural claims that have so far survived testing rather than the category of claims that have not yet been tested.
