## What the Universe Sounds Like

There is a question in mathematics that became famous in the 1960s with a title even non-mathematicians could not resist: *Can you hear the shape of a drum?* The mathematician Mark Kac, who posed it, was asking something specific. If you handed a blindfolded listener a recording of every tone and overtone a drum could produce, could she work out — from the sounds alone — what shape the drumhead was? Round or square? Big or small? Stretched tight or loose?

The full answer turned out to be: not always, but almost always. There are some drums that sound exactly alike despite having different shapes — a result that took mathematicians thirty years to settle, and that produced beautiful counterexamples — but they are rare. Most of the shape of a drum is recoverable from the sounds it can make. Geometry encodes itself in vibration patterns to a remarkable degree, and the encoding is tight enough that the shape and the sounds are mathematically inseparable.

This is not a curiosity. The same mathematics applies to any object whose sound is produced by vibration — strings, bells, the chambers of musical instruments. Specify the geometry of the instrument and, before anyone has played a note, the music it can make is determined. The Pythagoreans knew the kernel of this in the 6th century BC, when they noticed that strings whose lengths sit in simple ratios produce harmonious notes; modern mathematics has spent two thousand years sharpening the observation. Shape determines sound. The determination is exact.

In the 1980s and 90s, a French mathematician named Alain Connes pushed the idea into a new place. He asked: what about objects whose geometry is so unusual that no ordinary picture can hold them? What if you stripped away the visualisable surface and described the geometry purely by *what sounds it makes*? You give up the drumhead-as-shape; you keep the drumhead-as-spectrum. The Pythagorean observation — shape determines sound — would then run in reverse: the sounds *are* the shape, and any geometry, no matter how exotic, can be described entirely through the algebra of its possible vibrations. Connes worked this out in mathematical detail. The toolkit that resulted has been one of the more powerful programmes in pure mathematics ever since.

The framework, it turns out, has been building toward this toolkit since Chapter 1. The universe-as-closed-three-sphere is, in this language, the *drumhead* of a particular instrument. The boundary expansion field |Φ_NTE|, whose strength sets the rate of cosmic expansion, is one of the modes this instrument can sustain. The framework's structural predictions — the dark matter ratio, the heavy partner that gives the neutrino its mass, the temperature at which physics first switched on, the small energy density of the vacuum — are the *music* the universe-instrument plays. Each prediction is one note. The notes are not chosen separately; they are forced together by the shape and material of the instrument, the way a piano's overtones are forced by the lengths of its strings.

Connes called his toolkit the *spectral triple* approach to geometry — *spectral* because of the spectrum of sounds, *triple* because to describe an instrument completely you need to specify three things. What kinds of vibrations the instrument can hold. What sustains them in the right way to be sounded. And how they propagate from one place to another. Specify those three pieces and the instrument is fully determined. The music follows.

The universe-instrument's three pieces have specific names that the next chapter will introduce. What matters here is that the universe is being described, in this approach, as an instrument with a specific shape whose music is what physics observes. The chord of predictions the framework makes is not a list of separate facts. It is the harmonic structure of one instrument — named one note at a time but produced by one shape, struck once.
