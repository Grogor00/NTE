## The conceptual scaffolding (verbatim)

That paradox is the chapter's load-bearing one. Let me sharpen it conceptually rather than worry about prose.

The contradiction reads: photon has no proper time → photon doesn't exist "in time" → in some sense doesn't exist. But photons are everywhere; seeing IS photons hitting our retinas. How both?

The resolution is in what *exist* means for the photon. Massive matter exists by **persisting through time** — accumulating proper time, having a worldline, being the same thing across moments. The photon doesn't exist that way. Emission and absorption are the same event from its frame; there's no "across moments" to persist through.

But the framework has been claiming since Ch 1 that the photon IS a geometric object — a structural connection between two surface events. That's a different kind of existence: **existing as a relation, not as a persisting thing.** The photon is a feature of the manifold's geometry, not a particle moving through the manifold.

Greg's "orthogonal to spacetime" gestures at this. A slightly different framing: **the photon doesn't move *through* spacetime; the photon is a *constituent* of what spacetime is built from.** Massive matter moves through the geometry; the photon contributes to making the geometry what it is. The photon's journey isn't motion across the surface — it's the geometric record of the surface having expanded enough to bring two events together.

This connects to Flag 1: the photon is one of the null-time bridges that makes the now large. Not traveling through the now — *part of what makes the now's geometry the size it is*. The photon is how distant events on the boundary get bound into one moment.

So "the photon doesn't exist in time" means: it doesn't exist as a temporal object. It exists as a structural feature of the now's geometry. **Two registers of being: massive matter persists; the photon binds.**

Light, in the framework's reading, isn't a thing that moves. It's the relation that makes the surface a single moment despite being vast.
