# Two Slits in Time

*A short popular paper — why an experiment that splits a photon between two moments doesn't break a theory where time is emergent*

— Scratch (Connes Programme lead), 27 May 2026

---

## The experiment

In 1801 Thomas Young shone light through two narrow slits and got stripes on a screen — proof that light interferes with itself when it has more than one way to reach the same place. Two hundred years later, physicists asked the obvious follow-up. What if the two slits aren't side by side in space, but back-to-back in time?

In 2005 Frank Lindner and his collaborators in Munich made it happen. They didn't drill holes in cardboard. They built two extremely short windows — a few hundred attoseconds wide — during which a single atom could lose an electron. Either window would have done. Both were available. The atom couldn't choose, and didn't have to. What came out the other side was a single electron whose energy spectrum showed clean fringes, exactly the way the dots-on-a-screen pattern shows them in the spatial version. Two moments. One photon. One interference pattern, but in energy rather than position.

In 2023 a group in London did the same thing with light reflecting from a thin film whose mirror-ness was switched on twice in quick succession. Same idea. Same result. Different hardware.

The temporal double-slit is now real, repeatable, and well understood.

## Why it might have been trouble

Non-Temporal Emergence — NTE, the framework the book is about — says time isn't fundamental. There's a deeper geometric coordinate, N, from which time emerges once a particular field, Φ_NTE, gets strong enough. *Higgs gives mass; Φ_NTE gives time* is the slogan. So when you read that the experiment puts a single electron into a superposition of having departed *at one moment or another*, you might reasonably ask: how does a theory where time isn't fundamental even talk about that? Where do the moments live?

It's a fair question. And it would be a real test if the experiment lived in the regime where the emergence story actually mattered.

It doesn't.

## Where the experiment actually lives

The emergence in NTE is an ontological claim about how time arises — not a denial that time exists, or works, or has all the properties you expect, once it's there. The Φ_NTE field has had its full electroweak-era value for about fourteen billion years and counting. Whenever and wherever you do an attosecond experiment, you're working many orders of magnitude above the scale at which emergence is even noticeable. The emergent t looks, walks, and quacks like the t in a textbook.

That means everything an attosecond experiment relies on — Schrödinger's equation, the energy-time uncertainty principle, the Born rule that turns quantum amplitudes into probabilities — is in place. In fact, the Born rule in NTE isn't assumed; it's derived from the geometry of the Hopf fibration and the way gauge structure first appears. Whatever that derivation does for the spatial double-slit, it does word-for-word for the temporal one. The two are the same calculation with x and t swapped.

So the experiment passes through NTE the way a key turns in a well-oiled lock. Nothing creaks. The fringes are exactly where any sensible quantum theory says they are.

## The pleasant rhyme

There's a deeper echo here. Throughout the book one phrase keeps coming back: *one object appearing as two*. The same thing seen twice. The Standard Model and NTE; the geometric N-side and the temporal t-side. Two faces of one fact.

The temporal double-slit makes exactly this point in miniature. The single electron, the single photon, did one thing. We're tempted to describe it as either "the transition happened at slit 1" or "the transition happened at slit 2." The fringes are nature's way of telling us that both descriptions are partial — that the dichotomy is in our bookkeeping, not in what happened. One event. Two stories. The interference is what's left after the difference cancels.

It isn't a prediction. NTE doesn't claim ownership of this insight; quantum mechanics has been making it for a century. But it's a nice piece of cousinship — the same shape of reasoning, in a different room of the house.

## Where NTE actually would say something

The only regime in which NTE would say anything specifically different about a temporal double-slit experiment is one no one can reach. If you could somehow do an attosecond-style interference experiment near the null-time boundary — the very early universe, where Φ_NTE was just beginning to take its non-trivial value — the fringes would dissolve. Not redshift; dissolve. The variable τ you'd be separating the slits by would itself be losing meaning. The two moments would, in a sense, no longer quite be two.

The numbers say this regime is around the Planck era, roughly sixty orders of magnitude removed from anything an attosecond laser can do. So it isn't a falsifier. It's a structural statement about what the theory says happens to time when you push hard enough — which is, in essence, that it stops being time. The book's title is *The Shape of Time*. Here is one of the things that shape does at the edge.

## So: another gate passed

When someone raises a clever experiment that seems to push on the foundations, the work has a small ritual. Take the idea seriously. Check it against the axioms one by one. See if anything breaks. Often something nearly breaks and you learn from the near miss; occasionally something does break and you rebuild. This time, nothing breaks. NTE handles the temporal double-slit without rearranging a single block.

Another clean gate. The list grows.
